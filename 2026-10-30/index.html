AI memory baked onto an ordinary chip

Chips that train neural networks inside their memory need tiny switches whose resistance can be nudged up and down in small, even steps. This team built ferroelectric hafnium–zirconium oxide switches directly on top of off-the-shelf commercial chips, and found that the baking temperature decides how even those steps are.

Alexander Flasby, Anwesha Panda, Nathan Savoia & Laura Bégon-Lours

Why it matters

Chips that learn inside their own memory could run AI with far less energy, but their analog memory switches have usually needed special factory runs to build. This team added ferroelectric hafnia switches on top of ordinary, as-delivered 180 nm chips without harming the transistors underneath, and showed that baking them at 500 °C instead of 450 °C makes their steps more even, lifting simulated on-chip training accuracy from 69% to 82%.

The paper

Back-End-Of-Line Integration of 2-Terminal Ferroelectric Hafnia Synaptic Weights on Commercial CMOS Process

Alexander Flasby, Anwesha Panda, Nathan Savoia & Laura Bégon-Lours (Integrated Systems Laboratory, ETH Zurich). arXiv:2610.37530 [cond-mat.mtrl-sci], submitted 27 Oct 2026. Read on arXiv · PDF

In plain language: a neural network is mostly a big grid of numbers called weights. In an “in-memory” AI chip each weight is a physical device whose electrical conductance is the number, so multiplying and adding happens right where the data is stored instead of shuttling it back and forth. Here each device is a 5 nm stack of hafnium and zirconium oxide layers (a ferroelectric: every tiny crystal grain holds an electric dipole that a voltage pulse can flip) with a 2 nm tungsten-oxide layer, sandwiched between TiN and molybdenum electrodes. The more grains point one way, the higher the conductance.

The authors grew these devices on top of finished commercial XFAB 180 nm chips, the “back end of line”. The heat needed to crystallise the oxide is the risk, yet the transistors still worked after a 500 °C anneal (saturation current changed by at most 6%). Switching was confirmed as ferroelectric three ways: current scales with area, a PUND test gives 2Pr = 22.1 µC/cm², and the switching voltage follows the Merz law. Devices annealed at 450 °C program below 1.8 V with small cycle-to-cycle variation, but their weight updates are abrupt: almost all of the change happens in a few middle pulses (peak slope 4.5–5.4). Annealing at 500 °C roughly halves that peak slope and allows 1.2 V operation. In simulated MNIST training with plain analog SGD, that smoother curve lifts accuracy from 69% to 82% once real device variation is included.

Also measured: device-to-device variability 1.8% (5000 µm² devices), rectification ratio 9 at ±1 V, retention drift of 7.5% (LRS) and 9.5% (HRS) over 2 hours, and wake-up after 1000 cycles that triples the dynamic range.

Limits: these are single devices on test structures; the 1-transistor-1-resistor cells and full arrays are future work. The accuracy gain comes from simulation, not on-chip training, and both chips used identical noise settings so only the curve shape differs. Mixed precision already reaches 97% on both, and 500 °C is above the usual back-end thermal budget, even though these 180 nm transistors survived it.

The maths

The paper’s normalised weight and the Merz-law check on switching kinetics:

w = Gnorm = (G − GHRS) / (GLRS − GHRS) 1/|Vc| grows linearly with log(twrite) (Merz law, Fig. 5b) peak slope = max |dw / d(n/N)| 450 °C: 4.5–5.4 500 °C: below 3 ideal linear: 1

Illustrative model used on this page: a straight line with weight β (the slope left near the ends) blended with a logistic S-curve of steepness k, scaled so each branch runs exactly 0 → 1:

Lk(x) = [σ(k(x − ½)) − σ(−k/2)] / [σ(k/2) − σ(−k/2)], x = n/N, σ(z) = 1/(1 + e−z) w(x) = β·x + (1 − β)·Lk(x) peak slope p = β + (1 − β)·(k/4) / [σ(k/2) − σ(−k/2)] (k solved from p by bisection; p = 1 → straight line) step at weight w: Δw(w) = (1/N) · dw/dx evaluated where w(x) = w (numerical inverse) noisy pulse: w ← clip(w ± Δw(w) + (c/√N)·ξ), ξ ~ N(0, 1), c = cycle-to-cycle noise training toy: requested Δ = η·(target − w), delivered = Δ · Δw(w)·N (+ noise)

Because the delivered step is the request times the local slope, a steep device behaves like a learning rate that is several times too big in the middle of its range and far too small near the ends. That is the intuition for why plain analog SGD suffers on the 450 °C devices, and why mixed-precision schemes, which do the fine bookkeeping in digital and only fire pulses when an update is big enough, hide the problem.

How to use it

  • Compare the presets: on “450 °C anneal” the curve jumps almost straight from 0 to 1 in the middle; on “500 °C anneal” it is a gentler S; “Ideal linear” is the straight dashed line. Watch the share of the range covered by the middle 20% of pulses drop from about 71% (450 °C) to 46% (500 °C) to exactly 20% (ideal).
  • Same request, different step: the lower-left plot shows that one pulse moves a steep device about 5 times further at w = 0.5 than an ideal one, but only a fraction as far near 0 or 1, where noise can swamp it. Drag “slope near the ends” to 0 to see a pure S-curve that nearly stalls there.
  • Train a weight: the lower-right plot asks the weight to follow a series of targets. At the default η = 0.45 the 450 °C device overshoots and keeps ringing around mid-range targets (error about 0.09), while the 500 °C device settles (about 0.03, close to the ideal grey line). Lower η to 0.2 and the 450 °C device looks fine, even fast, in the middle: the real problem is that no single learning rate suits every weight at once, and a network has thousands of weights spread across the range.
  • Add noise: raise the cycle-to-cycle noise and see the four repeated cycles spread apart and fewer pulses stay above the noise.
