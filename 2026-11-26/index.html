Welding flat molecular sheets together makes a film as strong as Kevlar and far tougher

Almost every plastic, including Kevlar, is made of long one-dimensional chains, and a chain carries load only along its own length. This team started instead from flat, two-dimensional polyaramid sheets, each a rigid molecular net. Stacked up, such sheets hold together only by hydrogen bonds, which makes the films stiff but brittle. The trick here is to graft short flexible "linker" arms onto the sheet edges, stack the sheets into a film, and then heat it to 280 °C so the arms bond covalently to the next sheets. That "welds" the stack into one network. Below, a live model of the paper's microprojectile impact test shows what that does to a supersonic glass bead.

Why it matters

Body armour, spacecraft shielding and protective films are limited by how much impact energy each kilogram of material can soak up, and the best polymers such as Kevlar are built from one-dimensional chains that pass all of the load through a few bonds at a time. By covalently welding flat 2D polyaramid sheets into one network, these nanofilms match Kevlar 29's stiffness and strength (69.8 GPa and 3.9 GPa) and absorb up to ten times more energy per kilogram than Kevlar composite against supersonic microprojectiles, reaching 5.2 MJ per kg in the thinnest films.

In plain language: in a stack of hydrogen-bonded sheets, load has to cross from one sheet to the next through fragile hydrogen bonds, and those bonds are frozen in place, so the film cracks instead of giving. Welding adds covalent bridges between neighbouring sheets. The bridges share the load, which raises the stiffness from 21.4 to 69.8 GPa and the breaking strength from 0.9 to 3.9 GPa. They also act as soft springs. Under a hit, the sheets can slide past each other with friction (which burns energy) without falling apart, and the linkers finally snap one region at a time.

To measure toughness at extreme speed, the team fired single 8.11 µm silica beads (2.2 g cm⁻³) with a laser pulse at free-standing films in vacuum, at 300–1,200 m s⁻¹. They filmed each bead's speed before (vi) and after (vr) it passed through. The energy the film took out of the bead, divided by the mass of film in the bead's path, is the specific dissipated energy (the paper's Eqs. 10–12):

Ep = ½ mp (vi² − vr²),    Ep* = Ep / (ρ h Ap) = vi²/2 + Ed*

Here mp is the bead mass, ρ and h the film density and thickness, and Ap the bead's cross-section. The vi²/2 term is the energy needed just to shove the film "plug" up to the bead's speed. Ed* is everything else the material dissipates: stretching, sliding, cracking and bond breaking. Across 332 impacts on 14 films, welded films reached about 3 MJ kg⁻¹ near 1,000 m s⁻¹, roughly four times the unwelded film (0.53 MJ kg⁻¹ at 600–800 m s⁻¹). Films 33–38 nm thick reached 5.2 MJ kg⁻¹, more than the energy that TNT releases per kilogram (4.2 MJ kg⁻¹), though here it is absorbed rather than released.

Try it: fire a silica microbead at a nanofilm

The bead is the paper's: 8.11 µm across, 2.2 g cm⁻³, so 0.614 ng. Film density is 1.54 g cm⁻³, the middle of the paper's flotation bracket (1.48–1.595). For each film, Ep* at your speed comes from a trend line through the values quoted in the paper (see "Demo maths" at the bottom). The demo then inverts Eq. 10–12 to get the energy taken from the bead and its exit speed. If the film can absorb all of the bead's kinetic energy, the bead is caught.

Fibres are strong in only one direction. The welded film is just as strong in every direction within its plane, it can be spin-coated or slot-die coated from solution, and it scales up in area. Other measured properties: it keeps 77% of its stiffness after 1 MeV proton doses far above a year in low Earth orbit. Its hydrogen permeability is three orders of magnitude below EVOH, the benchmark barrier polymer, and its through-plane heat conduction is 64% higher than the unwelded film's (0.25 → 0.41 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹).

How to use it

  • Start at the defaults: a welded film 300 nm thick hit at 800 m s⁻¹. The film takes about 30% of the bead's energy, but the bead still gets through. Raise Stacked layers. The paper found that laminated films keep the same specific energy, so stacking about four layers (≈1.2 µm in total) catches the bead.
  • Switch to Unwelded 2DPA-L4. The same film is now about four times weaker per kilogram, the bead punches through much faster, and you would need roughly four times as much film to stop it.
  • Drag the thickness below 60 nm. The paper found the thinnest welded films (33–38 nm) were the toughest per kilogram, up to 5.2 MJ kg⁻¹, which it links to amide dipoles lined up by the silica substrate. The demo blends between the two trend lines from 60 nm down to 35 nm.
  • On the speed chart, compare the orange line with the grey boxes for Kevlar composite, steel, polystyrene and multilayer graphene, each drawn over the speed range it was tested at. The welded film sits up to an order of magnitude higher.
  • The bottom-left chart is the practical question: how many grams of film per square metre stop this bead? Lower is lighter armour. It uses KE / (Ap Ep*), so a material with 5× the specific energy needs 5× less mass.

Demo maths: mp = 2.2 g cm⁻³ · (π/6) · (8.11 µm)³; Ap = π (4.055 µm)²; ρ = 1.54 g cm⁻³. Ep* is modelled as a straight line through the origin, Ep* = k·vi, fitted to the paper's quoted points. Welded, ≥ 60 nm: k = 3.0 kJ kg⁻¹ per m s⁻¹, which gives ≈2.1 MJ kg⁻¹ at 700 m s⁻¹ (≈4× unwelded) and ≈3 MJ kg⁻¹ at 1,000 m s⁻¹. Welded, ≤ 35 nm: k = 5.2 (5.2 MJ kg⁻¹ at ~1,000 m s⁻¹). Unwelded: k = 0.76 (0.53 MJ kg⁻¹ at ~700 m s⁻¹). The lines are linear in log(thickness) between 35 and 60 nm. Captured if k·vi·ρ h Ap ≥ ½ mp vi²; otherwise vr = √(vi² − 2Ep/mp). This is a simplification. For example, the paper's 665-nm film caught a bead outright at 646 m s⁻¹, while the trend line predicts it would get through at about 300 m s⁻¹, so real thick films can do better than shown. Reference boxes are ranges from the paper's Supplementary Table 4: Kevlar/PVP composite 298–422 m s⁻¹, 0.445–0.536 MJ kg⁻¹; stainless steel (2 µm) 450–1,000, 0.192–0.534; polystyrene (75–150 nm) 350–800, 0.40–1.50; multilayer graphene 600–900, 1.10–1.26; gold (14–52 nm) 880–900, 0.529–0.578; Ni60Ta40 metallic glass 185–540, 0.33–0.56. The stopping chart holds Kevlar composite and steel at the midpoints of their ranges (0.49 and 0.36 MJ kg⁻¹). Those materials were tested with different projectiles, so treat the comparison as indicative.
