Measuring brain myelin more steadily by refitting the noise

Myelin, the fatty wrapping on nerve fibres, traps a little water between its layers, and that water loses its MRI signal much faster than the water elsewhere in the brain. Splitting a measured signal decay into its fast and slow parts gives the myelin water fraction, but the split is so sensitive to noise that the answer jumps around from scan to scan. This paper fits the decay, reshuffles the leftover noise hundreds of times, refits each copy and averages the results, which makes the number both more accurate and more repeatable, and gives an error bar for every voxel.

Erick J. Canales-Rodríguez, Thomas Yu, Juan Manuel Górriz, Santiago Galdo-Álvarez, Tobias Kober, Tom Hilbert, Gian Franco Piredda, Miguel Ángel Rivas-Fernández

Why it matters

Myelin water fraction is one of the most direct MRI measures of myelin, which is what multiple sclerosis and many other brain and nerve diseases damage, but noisy estimates make it hard to trust a change in one patient between scans. Averaging bootstrap refits cut the error by 36 to 41% compared with the standard fit in simulations and gave the most repeatable scan-rescan values in two groups of healthy volunteers, without any training data or extra scan time.

The paper

Improved Myelin Water Fraction Estimation and Uncertainty Mapping in Multicomponent T2 Relaxometry via NNLS Bootstrap Aggregation

Erick J. Canales-Rodríguez, Thomas Yu, Juan Manuel Górriz, Santiago Galdo-Álvarez, Tobias Kober, Tom Hilbert, Gian Franco Piredda, Miguel Ángel Rivas-Fernández (University of Granada, Siemens Healthineers and others). arXiv:2611.14395, November 2026. Read on arXiv · PDF · Code

In plain language: a multi-echo MRI scan samples the signal at N echo times. Each tissue water pool decays with its own relaxation time T2, so the signal is a sum of decaying exponentials, written as a non-negative spectrum x over a grid of P relaxation times:

y = A·x0 + ε,   x0 ≥ 0,   Aij = e−TEi/T2,j

The usual fit is non-negative least squares (NNLS): find x ≥ 0 that minimises ‖A·x − y‖². Nearby relaxation times give almost identical decays, so small noise moves weight between them, and the myelin water fraction (the share of the spectrum below 40 ms) wobbles. NNLS-BB keeps NNLS but runs it many times. It takes the residuals r = y − A·x̂, centres them and scales each by 1/√(1 − hii) using the leverage of the fitted columns, draws B new noise vectors by resampling those residuals with replacement, refits each y* = A·x̂ + r*, drops the most extreme 1% of replicates, and averages:

x̂BB = (1/K) Σb x̂*(b),   MWFBB = (1/K) Σb MWF(x̂*(b)),   error bar = SD over b

This page runs a Lawson–Hanson NNLS and the residual bootstrap live in your browser on the paper's grid of 60 log-spaced T2 values from 10 to 2000 ms and the 40 ms cutoff. To keep it fast and simple, the decay uses plain exponentials instead of the paper's stimulated-echo (EPG) model with flip-angle correction, there are 32 echoes 10 ms apart, the noise is Gaussian with SNR measured against the first-echo signal, and the two water pools are made-up log-normal peaks.

How to use it

  • See the wobble: press New noise draw a few times. The orange single-fit spectrum hops between spiky shapes and its MWF jumps, while the blue average stays close to the grey truth.
  • Read the error bar: the ± value is the spread of the refits, shown as the histogram. It grows when you lower the SNR or bring the two T2 peaks closer, which is exactly when a doctor should trust the number less.
  • Mimic disease: press Demyelinated lesion to drop the myelin water to a few percent and see how hard a small fast peak is to pin down.
  • Check the claim: run the 200-voxel test at different SNRs. The paper reports 36 to 41% lower error than plain NNLS across SNR 50 to 1000 with its full model; this simplified demo shows the same direction, and the exact size depends on the settings.
