Why some pores in a sodium battery never fill

Sodium-ion batteries store much of their charge by packing sodium into tiny closed pores inside a hard-carbon anode, and that filling is usually pictured as one flat "plateau" on the voltage curve. This paper combines first-principles calculations with a grand-canonical Monte Carlo model of a single pore and finds instead that every pore size fills at its own voltage (roughly 0.28 V divided by the diameter in nm), that big pores need a large sodium seed before they fill and so can stay empty at real charging speeds, and that wall defects speed up the seeding but slow down the final filling. Below, the paper's lattice model runs live in your browser.

Ihor Radchenko, Andrés Felipe Usuga, Yuxin Tang, Oleksandr I. Malyi

Why it matters

Sodium-ion batteries are a cheaper, lithium-free option for grid storage and low-cost cars, and most of the charge in their hard-carbon anodes sits in closed pores, which designers have tried to make as large and numerous as possible. This paper shows that pores larger than a few nanometres may never fill at realistic charging speeds and that wall defects help only up to a point, so the useful target is a window of pore sizes and defect levels that fills fast, not the largest possible pore volume.

In plain language: the pore is a ring of carbon wall sites around empty lattice sites. Sodium atoms can enter or leave only through sites touching the wall, where they trade with the sodium counter-electrode, and can hop between neighbouring sites inside. The energy of a configuration is a sum of pair interactions, ordered as the paper requires, |ENa–C| < |ENa–Na| < |ENa–defect|:

H = εNaNa·(Na–Na neighbour pairs) + εC·(Na–wall contacts) + εD·(Na on defect sites)

The applied voltage V sets how eager sodium is to come in, by shifting the chemical potential of the electrode below that of sodium metal:

μNa(electrode) = μNa(metal) − eV   ·   P(accept insertion) = min[1, exp(−(ΔH − μNa)/kT)]

Removals and hops use the same Metropolis rule. The fully filled pore is favourable whenever V is below its filling voltage, which the paper finds scales as Vfill ≈ 0.28 / d (d in nm). The catch is the path there: half-filled pores are unstable, so a sodium cluster has to nucleate first, and in big pores that seed must be large. The paper's time to fill therefore depends on the undervoltage, how far V sits below the pore's filling voltage, and defects change both the seeding (faster) and the final wall-wetting and diffusion stage (slower, because defects pin sodium to the wall).

From one pore to a whole electrode

A real anode holds a spread of pore sizes. If each size fills at Vfill = 0.28/d, the "plateau" is really a gentle slope made of many small steps; and pores too large to fill in the charging time add nothing. Pick a pore-size distribution (by share of pore volume) and the largest pore that still fills in time; the sweep above helps you choose that cut-off.

DFT on carbon-nanotube model pores (1.2, 1.3 and 1.6 nm) shows that even defect-free pores store sodium once van der Waals forces are included, that a finite sodium cluster is needed before filling is stable, and that smaller pores fill at higher voltages. The Monte Carlo runs use 2200 K to speed sampling, so times are relative, not seconds. The authors conclude that total closed-pore volume alone does not predict usable plateau capacity.

How to use it

  • Compare sizes: "Small pore" fills within a thousand or two sweeps; "Large pore" sits at a thin wetting layer for a long time, because it needs a big sodium seed before filling takes off.
  • Raise the voltage: filling slows as V gets close to Vfill and stops above it. That is why charging cut-offs above 0 V (used to avoid sodium plating) can leave large pores unused.
  • Add defects: the seeding stage (time to 30%) gets faster and Vfill goes up, but defects pin sodium to the wall, so the final stage can get slower. Run the defect sweep to see the two stages pull in opposite directions.
  • Scale up: in the last panel, move the cut-off below the typical pore size and watch the usable capacity collapse, even though every one of those pores would hold sodium in the thermodynamic limit.
