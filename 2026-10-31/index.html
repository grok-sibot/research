Warning when a typhoon is about to explode

Some tropical cyclones jump by 30 knots or more of wind in a single day, often just before landfall, and forecasters struggle to see it coming. This team first worked out which ocean and air conditions actually cause that jump, then made a deep-learning model respect those causes while it learns from satellite images.

Xinrui Li, Yijun Jin, Yifan Dong & Shoujuan Shu

Why it matters

Rapid intensification turns a manageable storm into a catastrophe within a day, and it is the part of a cyclone forecast that coastal evacuations most often get wrong. On five unseen typhoon seasons (2020–2024) this causally guided model more than doubled the probabilistic skill of the statistical schemes it was tested against (Brier skill score 0.259 versus at most 0.105), catching 68% of explosive days while raising a false alert on fewer than one in ten quiet ones.

The paper

Causal-prior-constrained deep learning for rapid-intensification warning of western North Pacific tropical cyclones

Xinrui Li, Yijun Jin, Yifan Dong & Shoujuan Shu (Zhejiang University, Hangzhou). arXiv:2610.40304 [physics.ao-ph], submitted 29 Oct 2026. Read on arXiv · PDF

In plain language: “rapid intensification” (RI) means a storm’s top sustained wind rises by at least 30 knots within 24 hours. It happened in only 7.39% of the six-hourly records in the test seasons, which is what makes it so hard to forecast: a model that always says “no” is right 93% of the time and useless.

The authors took 522 western North Pacific cyclones from 2000–2017 and ran a causal-discovery method (PCMCI+) over the weather and ocean fields around each storm. After bootstrap, refutation and directed-path checks, eight drivers survived with a trusted direction of effect on the next day’s wind change. Warm, deep ocean water both under the storm and around it, and moist low-level air, push intensification up. Strong wind shear, and several signs that the storm is already well organised and strong (high potential vorticity, a warm core, spin at 850 hPa, rising air at 500 hPa), push it down, partly because a mature storm has less room left to grow. Their model, CausalVisionRI, feeds infrared satellite imagery plus these fields into a neural network, and adds a training penalty whenever the network’s response to a driver goes against its trusted sign. Five copies were averaged into an ensemble and scored once, on 3,004 records from 131 storms in 2020–2024.

How to use it

  • Feed the storm: pick “Young storm over deep warm water”. The two ocean-heat bars dominate and the chance of a 30 kt jump climbs to about eight times the average. Then drag wind shear up to +2 SD and watch the chance roughly halve: shear tilts the storm and tears its core apart.
  • Why do spin and warm core point down? Try “Already mature”. A storm that already has a strong warm core and high vorticity is near its limit, so it usually gains less in the next day. The paper’s estimates say this, not that those features are bad for storms.
  • Set the alarm: in the second demo, slide the false-alarm rate. At 0.095 the causal model catches about 68% of explosive days, matching the paper. Switch to the SHIPS-12 statistical baseline at the same false-alarm rate and you catch noticeably fewer. Push the slider right and count how many false alarms each extra catch costs.
