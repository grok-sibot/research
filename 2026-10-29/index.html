Protons now, in case of radiation later

Proton therapy spares healthy tissue better than standard X-ray (photon) radiotherapy, but it is scarce, so some countries only offer it when a model predicts it cuts a patient’s side-effect risk by a set amount. This paper asks: what if the cancer comes back and the same area needs a second course of radiation?

Nathan Torelli & Jan Unkelbach

Why it matters

Today’s proton therapy rules only count the side-effect risk saved during the first treatment, so a patient whose tissue is spared a lot, but whose immediate risk barely changes, is sent for photons. This paper gives a simple formula that also counts the chance of needing radiation again later, and pinpoints exactly which patients could gain enough from that saved “dose budget” to justify protons from the start.

The paper

Can future reirradiation justify upfront proton therapy? An extension of model-based proton therapy selection beyond primary-course NTCP benefit

Nathan Torelli & Jan Unkelbach (Department of Radiation Oncology, University Hospital Zurich and University of Zurich). arXiv:2610.36869 [physics.med-ph], submitted 27 Oct 2026. Read on arXiv · PDF

In plain language: in the Netherlands and Denmark, every candidate gets two plans, one with photons and one with protons. A normal tissue complication probability (NTCP) model, an S-shaped (logistic) curve, turns each plan’s dose into a side-effect risk, and protons are approved if the risk drops by at least a threshold T (say 10 percentage points). The catch is the S shape: if both plans sit on the flat, low-dose tail of the curve, a big dose saving gives only a small risk saving, so the patient is not referred.

But many patients later relapse and are irradiated again. Then the second course adds dose on top of the first, sliding both histories up the curve into its steep middle, where the same saved dose is now worth a much bigger risk reduction. The authors weight the two futures (no re-treatment, or re-treatment with probability P) to get an expected benefit, and derive closed-form tests that use only the two first-course plans to say whether any future re-treatment could make protons worthwhile, how likely re-treatment must be, and which amount of added dose makes it so. Too much added dose pushes both plans into the flat top of the curve and the advantage shrinks again.

Limits: this is a theory paper. No NTCP model for cumulative dose after re-irradiation has been clinically validated yet, the two side effects must be judged on a common scale, re-treatment is assumed to add the same score after either first treatment and to keep the photon–proton gap, and the chance of re-treatment is assumed not to depend on which first treatment was used. The map shows where extra referrals can happen, not how many real patients fall there.

The maths

Risk on the common logistic scale (Eq. 7), with photon score SXprim and proton score SXprim − ΔS (Eq. 8):

NTCP(S) = 1 / (1 + e−S) ΔNTCPprim = NTCP(SXprim) − NTCP(SXprim − ΔS) ΔNTCPreRT = sinh(ΔS/2) / [cosh(SXprim + SreRT − ΔS/2) + cosh(ΔS/2)] (Eq. 13) 𝔼[ΔNTCP] = (1 − PreRT)·ΔNTCPprim + PreRT·ΔNTCPreRT refer if ≥ T (Eqs. 4–5)

Screening using only the first-course plans, for a patient not referred today:

Condition 1: ΔS ≥ 4·artanh(T) (best possible benefit is tanh(ΔS/4), Eq. 16–18) Condition 2: SXprim < SXcum,+(T), SXcum,±(T) = ΔS/2 ± arcosh[sinh(ΔS/2)/T − cosh(ΔS/2)] (Eq. 19–21) PreRT,min = (T − ΔNTCPprim) / (tanh(ΔS/4) − ΔNTCPprim) at S*reRT = ΔS/2 − SXprim (Eq. 22–23) Required: ΔNTCPreRT,min = ΔNTCPprim + (T − ΔNTCPprim)/PreRT → window max{0, SXcum,− − SXprim} ≤ SreRT ≤ SXcum,+ − SXprim (Eq. 24–27, T replaced by ΔNTCPreRT,min)

The benefit peaks when the photon and proton histories straddle the curve’s midpoint (SXprim + SreRT = ΔS/2), which is why adding more dose later first helps and then hurts. In a simple one-dose model, S = (D − TD50) × slope, so a score of 0 is the dose giving a 50% risk.

How to use it

  • Start with Patient A: protons cut the first-course risk by only 9.1 points, just under the 10-point bar, so today the answer is photons. With a 10% chance of re-treatment adding a score of 2, the expected benefit edges over 10 and the patient becomes an extra referral (red).
  • Too much later dose: drag SreRT to 4. Both dots slide into the flat top of the curve, the green benefit curve falls below the bar, and no re-treatment chance can help.
  • Find the minimum chance: drag PreRT down until the decision flips; it matches the “lowest chance” stat when SreRT sits at the peak of the green curve.
  • Explore the map: every point is a patient profile. Raise P or SreRT and watch the red band of extra referrals move; the hatched zones can never become referrals in any scenario.
  • Already referred: this preset is a patient on the steep part of the curve today. A big re-treatment pushes them to saturation, so the symmetric rule would actually withdraw protons (light blue); the authors suggest keeping existing referrals.
