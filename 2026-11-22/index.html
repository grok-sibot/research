The shape of the uptake curve decides the best carbon-capture sponge

Metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) are crystals full of tiny pores that can soak up CO2 and let it go again when you lower the pressure or heat them. There are thousands of known MOFs, and choosing one has usually meant ranking them by a single number: how strongly they grab CO2 at very low pressure. This paper computes the whole uptake curve for thousands of real MOFs from first principles, and shows that the curve's shape, not just the grip, decides which material suits which capture process. Below, a simple model of the four shapes runs live, together with the paper's working-capacity and regeneration-energy formulas.

Anna T. Bui, Grant M. Rotskoff, Thomas E. Markland

Why it matters

Catching CO2 from flue gas or air with porous sponges could use less energy than today's amine scrubbing, but picking the right sponge out of thousands has meant slow experiments or simulations too expensive to run with accurate quantum-level forces. This method gets complete first-principles CO2 uptake curves for thousands of real MOFs in less GPU time than standard simulation, and shows that each capture process needs a different curve shape, with every top-10% material for both heated swings regenerating below amine scrubbing's 3.6 GJ per tonne of CO2.

The paper

Characterizing adsorption thermodynamics in carbon capture materials: an efficient first-principles approach

Anna T. Bui, Grant M. Rotskoff and Thomas E. Markland (Department of Chemistry, Stanford University, USA). arXiv:2611.24759 [cond-mat.mtrl-sci], submitted 18 November 2026. Read on arXiv · PDF

In plain language: the usual way to get an uptake curve (an isotherm: how much CO2 the MOF holds against pressure) is a grand canonical Monte Carlo simulation, which keeps adding and removing molecules until things settle. With accurate machine-learned quantum-level forces, that is far too slow to run for thousands of MOFs. The authors instead train a neural classical density functional for CO2 once, from molecular-dynamics data. For any MOF you then only need the CO2–framework energy on a 3-D grid (from a foundation interatomic potential such as UMA-m). Solving one self-consistent equation gives the CO2 density in the pores, and from that the uptake at any pressure and temperature.

They sort the curves by their log-slope, which equals 1 when uptake grows in proportion to pressure (Henry's law), falls toward 0 as sites fill, and rises above 1 when CO2 molecules attract one another and pile in together:

s(P) = d ln n / d ln P

This gives four classes, tested in this order. Two-step: s falls and then rises again. S-shaped: s clearly exceeds 1 at some pressure of 1 bar or more. Otherwise the curve is Langmuir-like. It is steep Langmuir if a dual-site Langmuir fit, n = q1b1P/(1+b1P) + q2b2P/(1+b2P), needs a strong site that binds more than 50 times as tightly (b1/b2 > 50, about 10 kJ mol−1) and holds more than 20% of the capacity. Everything else is gradual Langmuir. A swing's figure of merit is its working capacity, and the heat of adsorption comes from Clausius–Clapeyron (paper Eq. 7):

Δn = n(Pads, Tads) − n(Pdes, Tdes),    Qst(n) = −R (∂ ln P / ∂(1/T))n

For heated swings, the energy to release one tonne of CO2 (paper Eq. 8) is the heat to pull the CO2 off plus the heat to warm the framework, shared over the CO2 released:

Eregen = Qdes + Qsens/Δn,    Qdes = (1/Δn) ∫ndesnads Qst(n) dn,    Qsens = ∫TadsTdes cp dT

Try it: build a MOF and run four swings

The demo stands in for the paper's neural cDFT with a toy two-component isotherm. One part is a binding site (Langmuir, energy Q1, capacity q1). The other is the pore (energy Q2, capacity q2), whose CO2 molecules can also attract each other with strength ω (Fowler–Guggenheim mean field): b2P = θ/(1−θ)·e−ωθ/RT. Both use b = b0eQ/RT with b0 = 10−6 bar−1. The classification, Clausius–Clapeyron Qst, working capacities and Eregen are computed exactly as the paper defines them, but on this toy curve. The parameters are illustrative, not fitted to any real MOF.

What the paper found

  • The authors screened the 5,591 experimentally made MOFs in the CoRE MOF 2025 database. With UMA-m, the most accurate potential, they computed 4,004 of them (72%).
  • Against measured isotherms of 20 well-known MOFs, UMA-m matched experiment to within a factor of about 1.5. uMOF-MH came close at about a tenth of the cost. The classical UFF force field underestimated uptake 5–7 times in MOFs with strong binding sites, such as Mn-MOF-74, CALF-20 and NOTT-300.
  • Even counting the energy-grid calculation, a cDFT isotherm takes less GPU time than grand canonical Monte Carlo.
  • At 10 bar, MOFs with the same Henry constant can differ in uptake by nearly a hundredfold, so ranking by the Henry constant alone cannot tell the four mechanisms apart.
  • Steep Langmuir MOFs have strong sites (median 40 kJ mol⁻¹) in narrow pores (about 4.2 Å). Gradual Langmuir MOFs have similar pores (4.6 Å) but weaker sites (28 kJ mol⁻¹). S-shaped MOFs have the weakest sites (20 kJ mol⁻¹) in wider pores (6.6 Å). Two-step MOFs combine strong sites (38 kJ mol⁻¹) with large cavities (9.6 Å). Most MOFs are gradual Langmuir, and about 13% are steep Langmuir with UMA-m.
  • Swings tested: VSA 0.1 → 0.01 bar and PSA 10 → 0.1 bar at 298 K, TSA 298 → 373 K at 0.1 bar, and PTSA 10 bar/298 K → 0.1 bar/373 K. Steep Langmuir MOFs win VSA and TSA. S-shaped and two-step MOFs win PSA and PTSA. Gradual Langmuir MOFs, the majority, are rarely top performers.
  • For TSA and PTSA, every MOF in the top 10% by Δn regenerates below the 3.6 GJ t⁻¹ amine-scrubbing benchmark. About half of all MOFs beat it in TSA, and nearly all do in PTSA, although that comparison ignores the work of compressing the feed to 10 bar. Mg-MOF-74 is the top TSA candidate.

Limits the authors note: working capacity is only a first screening metric (full process models rank materials better), the frameworks are treated as rigid, and the CO2 is pure, so N2 and water are not yet included.

How to use it

  • Tap a preset to load one of the four isotherm shapes, then watch the class label, the log-slope curve and the bar chart change.
  • Pick a swing to mark its adsorb (●) and release (○) points on the isotherm. The vertical gap between them is the working capacity Δn.
  • Compare Steep Langmuir and S-shaped: the first wins VSA/TSA and the second wins PSA/PTSA, as in the paper's Fig. 5.
  • Raise ω to make CO2 molecules attract each other. The log-slope climbs above 1 and Qst rises as the pore fills (the paper sees this rise in IRMOF-1).
  • Lower cp or raise Δn to see the heating term Qsens/Δn shrink. This is why PTSA, with its far larger Δn, regenerates more cheaply than TSA.
  • The Henry constant KH = q1b1 + q2b2 is the usual screening number. Notice how different curves can share a similar KH.

Demo maths: toy dual-component isotherm (Langmuir site + Fowler–Guggenheim pore) solved by bisection; Qst by central difference of ln P at fixed n (±2 K); Qdes by Simpson's rule; Eregen in GJ per tonne = kJ per gram of CO2 (M = 44.01 g mol⁻¹). Class thresholds follow the paper's SI. All numbers in the stats come from the toy model, not the paper.
