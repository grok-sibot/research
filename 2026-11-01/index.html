Steering a radiation beam to follow a breathing tumour

Pancreas and liver tumours ride up and down with every breath. This team took a 3D MRI method fast enough to locate the tumour every 148 milliseconds, added a predictor that guesses where it will be a moment later, and used it to slide the radiation machine’s metal shaping leaves so the beam follows the target.

Tianyu Yang, William P. Donahue, Aniket Pramanik, Victor Murray, Can Wu, Ricardo Otazo & Grace Tang

Why it matters

Abdominal tumours can move more than a centimetre with each breath, so patients are currently asked to hold their breath, wear a compression belt, or accept a wider margin of irradiated healthy tissue. Using real-time 3D MRI with no implanted markers, this system kept the beam within 0.7–1.8 mm of a moving target and raised the dose agreement for a clinical pancreas plan from 84.5% to 99.3%.

The paper

Real-time volumetric MRI-guided MLC tracking: A feasibility study

Tianyu Yang, William P. Donahue, Aniket Pramanik, Victor Murray, Can Wu, Ricardo Otazo & Grace Tang (Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center, New York). arXiv:2610.37832 [physics.med-ph], submitted 27 Oct 2026. Read on arXiv · PDF

In plain language: an MR-Linac is a radiotherapy machine with an MRI scanner built in, so doctors can see soft tissue during treatment. The beam’s shape is set by a multileaf collimator (MLC), rows of tungsten “leaves” that slide in and out. Normally the leaves stay put while the tumour moves underneath them. Here, a technique called MRSIGMA (MR SIGnature MAtching) first learns 30 breathing “states” and their 3D tumour positions, then during treatment matches each new radial MRI readout to the closest state, giving a fresh 3D position every 148 ms.

Those positions are smoothed with an exponential moving average, then fed to a linear Kalman filter that tracks position, velocity and acceleration and predicts two imaging intervals (2 × 148 ms) ahead. The software turns the change between successive predictions into leaf speeds and sends them to the linac every 40 ms. On an MRI-compatible motion platform moving 15 mm peak to peak, with a 10 cm circular field and a real patient breathing trace, they measured:

Try it: track a breathing target

This simulator follows the paper’s pipeline step by step: noisy position every Δt → exponential moving average → constant-acceleration Kalman filter predicting 2Δt ahead → leaf speed = (new prediction − previous prediction) / Δt, applied at the next 40 ms control cycle. The noise level, smoothing weight, Kalman tuning, penumbra and the irregular breathing trace are illustrative choices, since the paper does not publish them; the dose part is a 1D slice through a 10 cm field, not film. Leaves are placed once after a 4 s filter learning period and then only receive speeds, as in the paper, so any miss at that moment stays as a small constant offset; the authors list feeding back actual leaf positions as future work.

Why predict? The cost of lag

If the beam simply copies where the target was τ seconds ago, a sine motion x(t) = (A/2)·sin(2πt/T) leaves a worst-case gap of exactly

errormax = A · sin(π τ / T)

With no prediction, τ is at least the imaging interval plus processing, about 300 ms here. The Kalman filter’s job is to cancel most of that delay. AAPM Task Group 264 recommends tracking latency under 500 ms.

How to use it

  • See the problem: choose “No tracking” and press Play. The target slides out from the centre of the fixed circle, and in the dose plot the field edges blur. About 85% of points pass gamma, close to the paper’s 87–88% for untracked delivery.
  • Just copy the last image: pick “Follow last image”. The beam now chases the target but trails by roughly 0.4 s, so the error is largest mid-breath when the target moves fastest. Shorten the breathing period to make it worse.
  • Predict: switch to “Kalman prediction”. The lag drops to about 0.1 s and the error to around 1–1.5 mm, the same range the paper measured. Watch the peaks and valleys: like the authors report, the filter overshoots where the target turns around.
  • Tune it: open Filter settings. More Kalman flexibility reacts faster but amplifies noise; heavier smoothing (lower α) adds lag. Raise Δt to see why fast imaging matters, and try irregular breathing.
  • Lag budget: in the second card, slide τ to see how quickly a delay turns into millimetres of miss for fast breathing.
