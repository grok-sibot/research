Cooling with magnets instead of rare helium

Some crystals hold tiny magnets that point every which way. Switch on a strong field and they line up, giving off heat; let the heat drain away, switch the field off, and the crystal gets colder. This team found that a simple tellurium oxide of gadolinium, Gd₂TeO₆, does this unusually well at a few degrees above absolute zero.

Abhijit Nayak, Gurunathan Angusamy, Kusampal Yadav, S. K. Panda, Devajyoti Mukherjee, Raivo Stern, Tanmoy Chakrabarty & Krishnamurthy Jyothinagaram

Why it matters

Quantum computers, sensitive detectors and low-temperature physics labs need to be cooled to a few kelvin or below, and the usual way relies on helium-3, a scarce and costly isotope made mainly from decaying tritium. Gd₂TeO₆ is an easy-to-make oxide powder whose magnetic cooling power under a 7 tesla field beats the commercial standard refrigerant GGG by about 43% per kilogram and 48% per cubic centimetre, which could make magnet-driven cryocoolers smaller and less dependent on helium.

In plain language: each gadolinium ion (Gd³⁺) carries a magnetic moment with 8 possible orientations (spin J = 7/2), and Gd₂TeO₆ packs two of them into every small, light formula unit. With no field, those orientations are random, which is a lot of entropy (disorder). A magnetic field lines them up and squeezes that disorder out as heat. Run backwards in a thermally isolated container, removing the field pulls heat out of whatever is attached, which is how an adiabatic demagnetization refrigerator (ADR) works.

The team measured magnetisation up to 14 T and heat capacity, then used the Maxwell relation ΔSM = ∫0H (∂M/∂T)H dH to get the entropy change. The neighbouring Gd ions barely interact (Weiss temperature −1.25 K) and the crystal shows no magnetic ordering down to 2 K, so nearly all of the disorder stays available.

The theoretical ceiling is 2R ln(2J+1) = 2R ln 8 = 34.57 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, or 64.25 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹ for Gd₂TeO₆, so 14 T reaches 98.8% of it. ΔTad is the paper’s indirect estimate from magnetisation and heat capacity, not a direct refrigerator measurement.

Try it: line up the magnets

This model treats each Gd³⁺ as a free spin J = 7/2 (g = 2) feeling a weakened field, with the interaction folded in Curie–Weiss style by replacing T with T + |θ|. Per ion, with y = gμBB / kB(T + |θ|):

S/R = ln[ sinh(4y) / sinh(y/2) ] − y·[ 4 coth(4y) − ½ coth(y/2) ]

Two ions per formula unit and a molar mass of 538.1 g/mol convert this to J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹. For ΔTad, the crystal’s vibrations are added as a Debye solid with the paper’s fitted Debye temperature of 398 K, and the demo finds the temperature where total entropy in the field equals the zero-field entropy you started with. The single-θ shortcut and Debye-only lattice are simplifications; the authors’ Einstein-mode weights are not used.

With the measured |θ| = 1.25 K, the model gives 23.1, 47.5, 54.8 and 63.0 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹ at 2.5 K for 2, 5, 7 and 14 T, close to the paper’s 21.4, 48.5, 54.8 and 63.5. Above about 8 K the temperature jump tracks the paper within a few kelvin. Below that, the model overshoots badly (about 30 K instead of 7.5 K at 2.5 K) because it keeps all of the zero-field disorder down to the lowest temperature, while the real crystal is already starting to settle and store magnetic heat there.

How it compares

How to use it

  • Feel the field: drag the field slider. Below about 2 T only a fraction of the spins line up; by 14 T nearly all of the 64.25 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹ ceiling is used, as the paper found.
  • Move in temperature: the effect is biggest at the coldest end, because heat jiggles the spins less there, so a given field lines up more of them.
  • Change the neighbours: raise |θ| to make the ions push on each other more. The entropy change drops, especially at low field, which is why weakly interacting, Gd-dense crystals like this one are prized.
  • Compare materials: switch the bar chart between per kilogram and per cubic centimetre. Gd₂TeO₆ is not the record holder, but it is well ahead of GGG on both counts.
