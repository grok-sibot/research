A pinch of silver so lithium can keep up

Solid-state batteries with a lithium-metal anode fail when current is drawn faster than lithium can flow back to the interface: empty atomic sites (vacancies) pile up there and grow into voids. This study screens 50 elements with quantum-mechanical calculations to find which ones, dissolved in lithium, raise the number of vacancies enough to carry more current.

Arseniy S. Burov, Nadezhda A. Ashikhmina, Nurlan Zhanabayev, Anton O. Boev, Aleksandra D. Radina, Ilya V. Chepkasov, Alexander G. Kvashnin & Dmitry A. Aksyonov

Why it matters

Lithium-metal solid-state batteries promise more energy and safer cells, but at room temperature without applied pressure a lithium anode on the garnet electrolyte LLZO fails at only about 0.28 mA cm⁻², because lithium cannot refill the interface fast enough and voids open up. Screening 50 elements, this study predicts that only silver, germanium and cadmium dissolved in lithium can lift that limit above 1 mA cm⁻², with about 9 at.% silver reaching an estimated 4 mA cm⁻², the industrial target, while keeping 1522 mAh g⁻¹ of capacity.

The paper

Vacancy-enhanced Li solid-solution anodes for solid-state batteries: computational screening enabling high critical current density

Arseniy S. Burov, Nadezhda A. Ashikhmina, Nurlan Zhanabayev, Anton O. Boev, Aleksandra D. Radina, Ilya V. Chepkasov, Alexander G. Kvashnin & Dmitry A. Aksyonov (Skolkovo Institute of Science and Technology; Ashikhmina: Moscow Institute of Physics and Technology). arXiv:2610.32938 [cond-mat.mtrl-sci], submitted 23 Oct 2026. Read on arXiv · PDF

In plain language: lithium atoms in the metal move by hopping into empty sites, and at room temperature only about 2 in 10 million sites are empty. When a battery pulls lithium out of the anode, the empty sites it leaves at the interface have to spread back into the metal. If they can’t spread fast enough, they merge into voids, the anode loses contact with the electrolyte, and the cell fails. The current where this starts is the critical current density (CCD).

A foreign atom that attracts vacancies (positive binding energy Eb) raises the total number of vacancies around it by a Boltzmann factor exp(Eb/kBT). The authors computed solution and binding energies for 50 elements with density functional theory, kept those that dissolve in lithium and attract vacancies, and then asked how much each could raise the CCD within its real solubility limit. Only silver, germanium and cadmium clear 1 mA cm⁻². They then checked the catch: vacancies trapped next to silver also find it harder to leave, which eats into the gain.

Try it: the silver trap

More vacancies only help if they can move. Each silver atom holds a vacancy with 0.13 eV of binding, and the authors’ nudged-elastic-band calculations show that escaping a silver neighbour costs 0.12 eV, against 0.04 eV for a normal lithium hop. Equation S18 combines the extra vacancies on the 8 sites around each silver atom with that escape penalty to estimate how much faster lithium diffuses overall. Check: at 300 K and Eesc = 0.12 eV this gives rD ≈ 1.45 at 1 at.% and ≈ 5.5 at 10 at.%. The paper’s Table S9 lists 1.46 and 5.60 (it rounds the escape factor to 0.045). So the thermodynamic gain of about 147 per silver atom shrinks to a net 1.5-fold speed-up at 1 at.% silver.

The demo recomputes every row live from Eb, cmax and the molar mass, and matches the paper to rounding. Other findings: only 19 of 50 solutes pass the solubility filter; machine-learning models trained on the DFT data could have removed 31 of the 50 before any DFT; and the classic Hume–Rothery rules for solid solutions would wrongly discard many solutes that really do dissolve in lithium.

Limits, stated by the authors: these are optimistic, thermodynamic estimates. They assume the CCD rises in proportion to the vacancy count, that solute dissolved at high temperature stays dissolved at room temperature, and that bound and free vacancies move equally fast. The silver trap analysis suggests the real gain is much smaller. Zinc, which experiments show helps, is missed by this screen, so another mechanism may also be at work. The authors call for molecular-dynamics simulations and experiments to confirm.

The maths

Vacancy boost from a dilute solute at atomic fraction c (simplified Lomer model, z = 1, Equation S7), with kB = 8.617 × 10⁻⁵ eV K⁻¹:

η(c, T) = 1 + c · exp(Eb / kBT)

The critical current density scales with the vacancy supply until the LLZO electrolyte itself becomes limiting (Equation S11):

Jcrit = min(η × 0.28 mA cm⁻², 23.2 mA cm⁻²)

Capacity with one electron per lithium atom (Equation S13), MLi = 6.94 g mol⁻¹ and F = 96485 C mol⁻¹:

Q = (1 − c) F / [3.6 ((1 − c) MLi + c MX)] mAh g⁻¹

The silver trap (Equations S16–S18), with ηb = exp(Eb/kBT) and EaLi = 0.04 eV:

fnn = 8c ηavg = 1 + (ηb − 1) fnn rD = (1 − fnn) + ηb fnn exp[−(Eesc − EaLi) / kBT]

Simplifications in this demo: the paper evaluates everything at 300 K. The temperature slider just re-evaluates the same formulas, holding binding energies, solubility limits, the 0.28 mA cm⁻² baseline and the electrolyte cap fixed, which real materials would not do. The paper quotes the electrolyte cap as both 22.1 and 23.2 mA cm⁻²; the demo uses 23.2 from its worked calculation. The ηavg values in Table S9 run slightly above this formula (for example 119.4 vs about 118.5 at 10 at.%).

How to use it

  • Start with silver: at its 9 at.% solubility limit the vacancy boost is about 14 and the estimated CCD about 4 mA cm⁻², with 1522 mAh g⁻¹ left. Slide c down until the CCD reads 1 mA cm⁻²: you need only about 1.75 at.% silver and keep about 3024 mAh g⁻¹.
  • Try germanium: each Ge atom boosts vacancies about 6000-fold, but only 0.1 at.% dissolves, so it tops out near 2 mA cm⁻². Push c past the limit to see the dashed “what if” line.
  • Compare the losers: gallium, rhodium, thallium and platinum attract vacancies but barely dissolve, so they stay at the 0.28 mA cm⁻² baseline. Magnesium dissolves very well but hardly attracts vacancies, and costs a lot of capacity.
  • Spring the trap: in the silver card, raise the escape barrier and watch the net speed-up rD fall far below the vacancy boost. Set it to 0.04 eV (no extra penalty) to see the optimistic case.
  • Change temperature: warmer lithium weakens the binding boost, so a solute’s advantage shrinks (an extrapolation; see the maths card).
