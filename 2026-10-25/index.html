Which metal mixes can hydrogen turn straight into alloy?

Instead of mining, smelting and melting each metal separately, you could blend their oxides and let hydrogen pull the oxygen out, leaving a finished alloy and only water as exhaust. This study works out, for thousands of cobalt–chromium–iron–manganese–nickel blends, how dry the hydrogen has to be for the last scrap of oxide to disappear.

Albert Linda, Shiv Shankar & Dierk Raabe

Why it matters

Metal production causes about 40% of global industrial greenhouse gas emissions, and hydrogen can turn a blend of oxides straight into an alloy in one step while giving off water instead of CO₂. This study maps how dry that hydrogen must be across 3,526 cobalt–chromium–iron–manganese–nickel blends: cobalt–iron–nickel mixes reduce easily (a median H₂/H₂O ratio of about 0.16 at 1100 °C), while manganese- and chromium-bearing mixes need gas hundreds to thousands of times drier, so recipes can be screened before anyone fires up a furnace.

The paper

Designing sustainable high-entropy alloys directly from mixed oxides: A thermodynamic perspective

Albert Linda, Shiv Shankar & Dierk Raabe (Max Planck Institute for Sustainable Materials, Düsseldorf). arXiv:2610.30870 [cond-mat.mtrl-sci], submitted 22 Oct 2026. Read on arXiv · PDF · Data & code

In plain language: every metal oxide holds on to its oxygen with a certain strength. Hydrogen can take that oxygen away (making water) only if the gas “pulls” harder than the oxide “holds”. How hard the gas pulls depends on the temperature and on the ratio of hydrogen to water vapour, r = H₂/H₂O. Drier gas (bigger r) pulls harder. The catch is that water builds up right where the reaction happens, so very large ratios are hard to keep.

In a blend, the metals dissolve in each other and the oxides mix, so each one holds its oxygen differently than it would on its own. The authors used CALPHAD thermodynamic databases (Thermo-Calc with TCOX10) to find, for each blend and temperature from 500 to 1500 °C, the last oxide that has to be removed, and the minimum gas ratio rmin that removes it. Manganese and chromium make that last oxide much harder to remove. Cobalt, iron and nickel make it easier. These effects can change size, or even reverse, depending on what else is in the mix. They also show that a blend with fewer in-between steps is not always easier to finish.

The maths

Hydrogen burning to water sets the oxygen potential of the furnace gas (T in kelvin, J per mol O₂). At a gas ratio r = pH₂/pH₂O:

2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O: ΔG°H₂O(T) ≈ −483,600 + 88T ΔGHyDR(T, r) = ΔG°H₂O − 2RT ln r

The last oxide in a blend has an onset energy ΔGonset (kJ per mol O₂). The reduction driving force and the minimum gas ratio follow from Equations 5 and 7:

ΔGrf = ΔGonset − ΔGHyDR/1000 rmin = exp[(ΔG°H₂O(T) − 1000 ΔGonset) / (2RT)]

Reduction is complete at equilibrium when ΔGrf > 0, that is, when r > rmin. The iron-oxide reference is ΔGFeO ≈ (−544,000 + 134T)/1000 kJ/mol O₂. Because rmin is exponential, small energy differences matter a lot. At 1100 °C, ±10 kJ/mol O₂ changes rmin by a factor of about 1.55, as the paper notes.

Check: at 700 °C and r = 3 the demo gives a gas line of −415.7 and an FeO line of −413.6 kJ/mol O₂, a margin of +2.1, matching Table 1. Family lines: ΔGonset(T) = ΔGHyDR(T, 3)/1000 + median ΔGrf, taken at 700 and 1300 °C and joined with a straight line. At 1100 °C this lands within a few kJ/mol O₂ of the paper’s own median rmin values (the open circles). The gap is small but noticeable on a log scale. The Co–Fe–Ni family is shown only at 1100 °C, because the paper gives its median as a number only at that temperature.

How to use it

  • Furnace temperature: heating lifts every oxide line toward the gas line, so hotter usually helps. Watch Fe–Mn–Ni: its median rmin falls from about 72,000 at 700 °C to about 71 at 1300 °C. Even so, it still needs much drier gas than plain iron oxide.
  • Gas dryness r: dragging it right lowers the blue gas line. Going from r = 3 to r = 10 gains only about 20–30 kJ/mol O₂. That helps blends near the edge, but it cannot close a gap of 100 kJ or more in manganese- or chromium-rich blends.
  • Recipes at 900 °C: tap the presets and compare. Co5Fe60Ni35 and Co10Fe10Ni80 turn fully to metal even in wet gas (r = 1). Co10Fe80Mn10 has only one oxide step, but it still needs r ≈ 4.75 × 10³. Fewer steps does not mean easier.
  • Design rule: to make an alloy straight from oxides with realistic hydrogen, keep manganese and chromium low or accept very dry gas and higher temperatures. Adding nickel or cobalt generally makes the last oxide easier to remove, though how much depends on the rest of the blend.
