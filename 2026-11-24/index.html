Hidden fate boundaries in the wind decide which way a typhoon turns

Sometimes a tropical cyclone's forecast tracks split into two branches, one heading for one coast and one for another. This paper says the split is caused by hidden lines in the steering winds, which the authors call fate boundaries. Storms that start on opposite sides of one of these lines, even 50 km apart, end up far apart. The authors find these lines as ridges of a new directional finite-time Lyapunov exponent. Below, their minimal two-high-pressure model runs live: move the storm, change the highs, and watch the ensemble split exactly where it straddles a ridge.

Wei Zou, Jizhou Li, Xurui He, Lei Liu, Pinaki Chakraborty, Lin Li

Why it matters

When a typhoon's forecast tracks split, emergency managers can't tell which coast to warn, and today's ensembles often show the second branch only a day or two before the turn, or miss it entirely. In all 61 strongly split forecasts among 1,179 Northern Hemisphere storms from 2011–2025, a fate boundary in the steering winds explained the split, and for Typhoon Khanun (2023) it revealed the surprise turn toward Japan three days before the ECMWF ensemble did.

In plain language: a tropical cyclone is pushed along by the large-scale winds around it, the steering flow. Forecasters run ensembles, many copies of the forecast started from slightly different storm positions. Usually the tracks fan out smoothly, but sometimes they split into separate branches. The textbook explanation is the col, a weak-wind gap between two competing high-pressure systems. The authors show that starting inside the col is neither necessary nor sufficient for a split. Because the winds change over time, what matters is where nearby paths end up, not the wind map at one moment.

So they borrow a tool from studies of pollution and oil-spill transport: repelling Lagrangian coherent structures, found as ridges of the finite-time Lyapunov exponent (FTLE). Release a tracer at every point x0, follow it for a window T to get the flow map Φ, and measure how fast neighbours stretch apart:

FTLE(x0) = (1 / 2T) ln λmax(C),    C = (∇Φ)T∇Φ

In the real atmosphere, plain FTLE also lights up wherever wind shear just stretches tracers along the same path. The paper's directional FTLE keeps only separations onto different headings. At each point it finds the direction n = ∇θ/|∇θ| in which the tracer's net heading θ changes fastest. It releases a pair x± = x0 ± (d0/2)n with d0 = 50 km and follows both:

dFTLE(x0) = |θ+ − θ−| · [ ln(dmin / d0) ]+,    dmin = min(d+, d−)

Here d+ is the closest distance from the end of the x+ path to anywhere on the x− path (and the reverse for d−). If one path just slides along the other, dmin stays small. Ridges of this field are the fate boundaries. The minimal model behind Fig. 2 uses two clockwise point vortices standing in for the continental and oceanic highs around Khanun, 2,000 km apart. Each has a core radius a = 200 km and circulation Γ0 = 9.3×107 m²/s. Between hours 8 and 24 the strengths ramp linearly to 0.6Γ0 (north-west) and 1.4Γ0 (south-east):

u(x, t) = Σi Γi(t) ẑ × (x − xi) / [ 2π ( |x − xi|² + a² ) ] + Vβ

For real storms (ERA5 and ECMWF fields), the storm's own vortex is removed and the wind is averaged over its depth. A constant beta drift Vβ is then added, the north-westward self-propulsion of a cyclone. The paper fits that drift per storm. Here it is an optional slider.

Try it: find the fate boundary, then straddle it

The map spans 4,800 × 4,000 km. The two highs (H) spin clockwise and are fixed in place. The shading is computed live on a 108 × 90 grid: a tracer and a 50 km probe pair at every point, integrated with 4th-order Runge–Kutta. Click or drag on the map (or focus it and use the arrow keys) to move the storm. Its ensemble members start scattered around it by the position error you set.

How to use it

  • Start with the defaults, the paper's Khanun-like set-up: the north-west high weakens to 0.6 and the south-east high strengthens to 1.4 over hours 8–24. The red ridge is the fate boundary. The storm starts inside the purple col, where the textbook says tracks should split, yet all members stay together, because the ridge runs about 200 km to the west.
  • Click Put storm on the boundary. The members straddle the ridge and split into two branches that end over 1,000 km apart. Then drag it a couple of hundred km off the ridge, even deeper into the col, and the split vanishes. Being in the col is not what matters; straddling the ridge is.
  • Switch to Standard FTLE. It highlights separation of every kind. Compare where it and the directional version disagree, and check which one predicts the split.
  • Make the two highs equal (both 1.0), or swap their trends. The boundary moves and reshapes, because it depends on how the flow changes during the window, not just on today's map.
  • Shrink the position error to 20 km near the ridge: a small ensemble can sit entirely on one side and miss the other branch, as happened to Khanun. Seeding members on both sides of the ridge, as the authors propose, would catch it.
  • Add beta drift (the paper fits 0–2 m/s per storm) and change T to see the boundaries move and sharpen with longer windows.

RK4 with a step of about 24 minutes (the paper uses 30 s). Headings are net displacements measured anticlockwise from east, and ∇θ is taken by wrapped central differences on the grid. The col is shaded where the steering wind at t = 0 is below 5 m/s (our choice; the paper shades it by eye). "Fate boundary" here means a directional FTLE of at least 40% of the field's 99th percentile; points within 300 km of a high's centre are ignored. Branches come from 2-means clustering of member end points. A split is counted when the centroids are more than 1,000 km apart (closer branches are usually one group smeared out by shear) and the smaller branch holds at least 10% of members. Beta drift splits the slider value equally between west and north. All numbers on this page come from the simulation, not the paper.
