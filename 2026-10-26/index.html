Can a power meter tell when an AI is being trained?

Training a big AI model makes thousands of chips work hard, then pause to share results, then work again, roughly once a second. This study shows that a sealed electricity meter outside a data centre can pick up that beat in five minutes of readings, even when the beat drifts or the operator tries to blur it.

Tom Kimpson, Mauricio Baker & Emlyn Graham

Why it matters

Any agreement to limit the largest AI training runs needs a way to check compliance without taking the operator’s word for it, and this study shows that a tamper-evident power meter on a data centre’s supply, sampling just twice a second, could do that job. On simulated facility power, the tracker caught at least 96% of training runs in five-minute windows at a 1% false-alarm rate, and the only disguise that often slipped past made training need 93–267% more compute to reach the same quality.

The paper

Detecting AI Training in Datacentre Power

Tom Kimpson (University of Melbourne; MATS), Mauricio Baker (University of Oxford) & Emlyn Graham (MATS). arXiv:2610.30182 [cs.CR], submitted 19 Oct 2026. Read on arXiv · PDF

In plain language: when many chips train one model together, each step has a busy, high-power compute phase followed by a quieter phase where the chips swap results. That rhythm, about once a second, adds a narrow “line” to the power spectrum. Answering a chatbot’s requests (inference) looks different: requests come and go at random, so their power is spread across many frequencies with no single line.

The catch is that a real training rhythm wanders. Slow chips, hot hardware and network hiccups speed it up and slow it down, and an operator who wants to hide can make it wander on purpose. A test that looks for one sharp peak then fails. The authors borrowed a trick from sonar and gravitational-wave astronomy: cut the trace into 16-second slices, see which frequencies stand out in each slice, and use the Viterbi algorithm to find the best smooth path through them. A drifting training rhythm leaves a bright path. Random inference noise does not. They tested this on synthetic data centre power traces built from a published model of training power, plus their own model of inference, and also checked eight ways an operator might try to hide.

Neither has been checked against a real multi-GPU facility meter yet. The tracker detects any steady, slowly wandering rhythm, so non-training loads with a training-like cycle, or a chip’s own power-management loop, can also trip it.

The maths

Each training step i has a period set by the base rhythm f₀, a slow drift gi and per-step jitter ξi (Equations 3–5, θ = 0.02):

Ti = 1 / [f₀ gi (1 + ξi)], gi − 1 = (1 − θ)(gi−1 − 1) + εi, σg = (σf/f₀)·√(2θ − θ²)

A fraction ρi of each period is at about full power and the rest is about 30% lower (Equation 2). The meter averages over τ, samples at fs, and adds 4 W of noise. The tracker cuts the mean-removed readings into 16 s Tukey-windowed slices (half overlap), takes each slice’s spectrum, and measures how far each bin in 0.3–1.7 Hz stands above the slice median:

Skj = |Σm w[m] x[jH+m] e−2πikm/M|² Lkj = ln Skj − ln mediank Skj

The score is the best path through that map, charged λ = 1 per bin of jump between slices, averaged over slices. Dynamic programming finds it exactly:

V = (1/Nt) maxk₀…kNt−1 [ Σj Lkj j − λ Σj |kj − kj−1| ] flag training if V > vα

The disguise draws G̃i = G₀ / max(1 + ζi, 0.05), ζi ~ N(0, σG²), rounded at random to an integer. With h(B) = B/(B + Bnoise), the extra data needed is

Δ = [h(G₀)/G₀] / [E h(G) / E G] − 1

Check against the paper: at the nominal meter this demo’s threshold comes out around 0.85–0.9 (the paper quotes vα ≈ 0.91), and inference-only traces score around 0.67 (paper example: 0.73). In offline runs of this exact code with 200 nulls and 100 training traces per setting, detection was 1.00 at σf = 0–0.4 Hz and 0.98 at 1.5 Hz. The matched filter scored 0.99, 0.60 and 0.32 at σf = 0, 0.1 and 0.4 Hz. Detection was 0.98 at τ = 0.5 s, 0.34 at τ = 1 s, and 0.49 under σG = 0.7, all close to the paper.

How to use it

  • Start honest: with a training run selected, the white path sits on the dashed true rhythm, and V lands far above the threshold. Switch to “Declared inference only”: the path still exists, because Viterbi always returns one, but it just strings together noise, and V falls below the line.
  • Make the rhythm wander: raise σf, then run 100 windows. The tracker keeps catching nearly everything, while the fixed-peak test, which expects one sharp spike, drops sharply.
  • Choose the meter: try 2 Hz (still works, partly because faster rhythms fold back into the band), then 1 Hz or 0.5 Hz (the rhythm falls above the Nyquist limit and detection collapses). An averaging window of 1 s puts a blind spot right at 1 Hz. The paper’s spec is at least 2 Hz with no more than 0.5 s of averaging.
  • Play the cheater: push σG to 0.7 and run 100 windows. About half the windows slip through, but the cost card shows the model then needs roughly 1.5–3 times the data. Over a full day of windows, the paper still flags most such runs.
