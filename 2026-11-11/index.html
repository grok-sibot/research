Running AI weather forecasts on half the bits

AI weather models such as Aurora and Pangu-Weather ship with 32-bit numbers and run that way by default, even though today's graphics cards do 16-bit maths much faster. This paper runs five public models in 16-bit half precision (fp16) on one consumer graphics card. The forecasts barely change, provided one rule is kept: the weather state itself stays in 32 bits, and only the normalised numbers that enter the network are rounded.

Stefan Siegert

Why it matters

Running a 10-day AI forecast in fp16 is 1.3 to 3.5 times faster and uses 1.4 to 3.7 times less energy, while global error changes by at most 1.4%. That is less than an hour of forecast lead time up to day 8. Researchers, forecasters in poorer countries and anyone running large ensembles on a single 16 GB card can do several times more forecasts for the same money and electricity.

The paper

Half-precision inference of AI weather prediction models: negligible loss of skill at a fraction of the cost

Stefan Siegert (Department of Mathematics and Statistics, University of Exeter). arXiv:2611.09573, 6 November 2026, not yet peer reviewed. Read on arXiv · PDF

In plain language: a 16-bit fp16 number keeps about 3 significant digits and cannot go above 65,504. bf16, the other 16-bit format, reaches as high as fp32 but keeps only 2 to 3 digits. Weather fields in physical units are big numbers. Sea-level pressure is about 101,000 Pa and upper-air geopotential is up to 208,000 m²/s², so in fp16 they become infinity. In bf16 they round coarsely: about 148 Pa for pressure and 0.57 K for temperature, which is about as large as a model's whole day-1 error, and the rounding happens again at every step.

The fix is to normalise first. In fp32, each field x is turned into a number close to 1 using its global mean μ and standard deviation σ. Only that number is rounded and fed to the network, and the output is turned back into physical units in fp32:

x̃ = (x − μ)/σ  →  network in fp16 on round16(x̃)  →  x = μ + σ·ỹ  (in fp32)

Rounded this way, the fields change 16 to 80 times less. Across 52 start dates in 2024, verified against ERA5, global RMSE moved by at most 1.4% (FourCastNet, z500 at day 1) and by about 0.5% or less for the other four models. No value inside any network came closer than 38 times below fp16's limit. bf16 was as fast but less accurate for every model. Simple 8-bit quantisation cost far more skill.

This demo does real fp16 and bf16 rounding (round to nearest, ties to even) in your browser. The table values, speed-ups and energy figures come from the paper's Tables 5 and 6. The means and standard deviations used for normalising, and the error-growth curve in the last panel, are rough illustrative choices for this page, not the paper's numbers.

Turning a small RMSE change into lost hours

A forecast's error grows with lead time. If fp16 raises the error at day t by a fraction δ, the fp16 forecast is as good as an fp32 forecast issued Δt hours later, with Δt ≈ δ·E(t) / E′(t). Error grows fast early and slowly later, so the same δ costs more hours late in the forecast.

How to use it

  • Break fp16: pick Sea-level pressure or z50. The raw fp16 row becomes ∞, because the value is past the red line at 65,504 on the plot. The normalised fp16 row stays accurate to a fraction of a pascal.
  • See why bf16 hurts: pick 2 m temperature and drag the slider. Raw bf16 jumps in steps of 1 or 2 K. On the plot, bf16's gap line sits about 8 times above fp16's at every size.
  • See what normalising does: the ◆ marker sits near 1 on the x-axis, where all three formats have tiny gaps. The ● marker is the raw value, out where gaps are large or fp16 has run out.
  • Count the savings: choose a model and set how many forecasts you need, for example 10,000 for a large ensemble study. Aurora drops from about 25 days of GPU time to about 7.
  • Convert error to hours: set δ to 0.2%, the size most models showed late in the forecast, at day 8, and about an hour is lost. Now set δ to the worst case of 1.4% at day 1: because error grows fast early on, that costs under an hour too. The paper does this conversion with its measured error curves, so the exact hours here are only illustrative.
