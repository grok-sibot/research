Why blood lingers in the heart's side pocket

The left atrial appendage is a small, finger-shaped pouch off the heart's left upper chamber, and in atrial fibrillation it is where about 90% of the clots that cause strokes form. Using blood-flow simulations of 17 real patients' hearts, with wall motion taken from 4D CT scans, this paper shows that plenty of blood crosses the pouch's mouth each beat, yet the pouch is cleared at only about a quarter to a half of the rate that this amount of exchange should give. The reason: blood near the mouth is pushed out and then sucked back in, while old blood at the tip stays put. Below, a reduced two-region version of the paper's washout analysis runs live.

Y. Stöcker, M. Guerrero-Hurtado, A. Gonzalo, S. Sharda, E. Durán, P. Martinez-Legazpi, A. M. Kahn, E. McVeigh, J. Bermejo, O. Flores, J. C. del Álamo, M. Garcia-Villalba

Why it matters

Atrial fibrillation affects tens of millions of people, and doctors judge their stroke risk and need for blood thinners or an appendage-closing device mostly from scores and simple measures such as how much the appendage squeezes or how much blood flows through its mouth. This paper shows that flow through the mouth does not tell you how well the appendage is actually cleared, because blood is often swapped back and forth near the opening while old blood stays at the tip, so better risk checks and treatments need to measure which blood is exchanged, not just how much.

In plain language: the team simulated blood flow in 17 patients' left atria (8 scanned in normal rhythm, 9 during atrial fibrillation), released a harmless "dye" that filled only the appendage, and watched how fast it was washed out. They split the flow through the appendage mouth (the ostium) into an active part, forced by the appendage changing volume, and a passive part, where blood goes in and out at the same time with no net change:

Qact(t) = |dVLAA/dt|   ·   Qpas(t) = Qin + Qout − Qact = 2·min(Qin, Qout)

If the appendage were perfectly stirred and every drop coming in were fresh, the dye would decay as

c̄m(t) = exp[ −∫ Qin(τ) / VLAA(τ) dτ ]

using either only the volume-driven inflow, max(dV/dt, 0) (rate kV), or the true inflow through the mouth (rate kQ). The real washout rate per cycle, kn = −ln[c̄(tn+T)/c̄(tn)]/T, splits exactly into three parts:

k = kQ + α + β,   α = −⟨(Qin/V)·(c̄in/c̄)⟩ ≤ 0,   β = ⟨(Qout/V)·(c̄out/c̄ − 1)⟩

α is the penalty for sucking back dye that was just pushed out (re-ingestion); β is negative when the blood leaving is cleaner than the appendage average, which happens when old blood hides at the tip (last in, first out).

What the paper found

Measure (17 patient-specific atria)Result
Total blood swapped through the mouth per beat (÷ appendage volume)0.94 to 3.67
Share of that driven by appendage volume change24–78% (24–53% in AF)
Appendage emptying fractionabout 13–65%
Actual washout vs well-mixed prediction with true inflow, k/kQ0.26–0.53
Re-ingestion penalty α/kQ−0.05 to −0.43
Outflow-is-cleaner penalty β/kQ−0.13 to −0.76 (bigger than α in most)
Effective washout rate k0.20 to 0.87 s⁻¹
Mean blood residence time in the appendage1.7 to 6.4 s

Both penalties were negative in every patient. Residence-time maps showed young and old blood side by side, and in one AF patient the old blood sat mainly in the distal tip. Patients with different anatomy and rhythm could end up with similar washout rates, so neither flow volume nor emptying fraction alone predicts how stagnant the appendage is.

How to use it

  • Start with "Perfectly stirred": no re-ingestion and fast stirring make the model curve for the whole appendage sit on the dashed well-mixed (true inflow) prediction, so k/kQ is close to 100%.
  • Turn down the stirring: the tip stays dark while the mouth clears; cleaner blood leaves, β grows negative and the washout rate drops even though the same volume crosses the mouth.
  • Turn up re-ingestion: the appendage sucks back blood it has just pushed out, so α eats into the ideal rate.
  • Compare "Normal rhythm" with the AF presets: a weak squeeze lowers the active share; swapping at the mouth can keep total exchange high, but the tip still holds old blood for longer, which is the stagnation linked to clots.
  • Read the waterfall: the first bar is what the flow through the mouth "should" achieve; the α and β drops are the two losses the paper identified; the last bar is what you actually get.
