Fast charging and where the lithium goes

Charging a lithium-ion battery faster is convenient, but at some speed the battery starts to wear out quickly. This study cycled off-the-shelf coin cells of two common chemistries 100 times at four charging speeds, then opened them up. Both kinds held up at 1C (a full charge in about an hour) and fell apart at 2C, and the cells charged at 2C had the most lithium stuck on the graphite anode.

Naisargi Kanabar, Nicholas Fusco, Seiichiro Higashiya, Devendra Sadana, Stephen Bedell, Haralabos Efstathiadis

Why it matters

Phones, laptops and electric cars are all sold on how fast they charge, yet this study shows a sharp cliff between 1C and 2C: commercial NMC811 cells went from keeping 99.7% of their capacity to 72.3% after just 100 cycles, and LCO cells fell to 11.1%. The damage shows up mainly as lithium piling up on the graphite anode, 8 to 20 times more than at slow charging, so charger designers and battery-management software should treat the anode as the limit that sets a safe charging speed.

The paper

Lithium redistribution and electrode degradation in commercial NMC/Graphite and LCO/Graphite cells under high-rate cycling conditions

Naisargi Kanabar, Nicholas Fusco, Seiichiro Higashiya, Devendra Sadana, Stephen Bedell, Haralabos Efstathiadis (University at Albany, SUNY). arXiv:2611.15818, November 2026. Read on arXiv · PDF

In plain language: a C-rate is charging current measured against the battery's size. At 1C the current would fill the cell in one hour, at 2C in half an hour, at 0.3C in about three hours. The team used one fresh 200 mAh NMC811/graphite cell and one fresh 45 mAh LiCoO₂ (LCO)/graphite cell for each of 0.3C, 1C, 2C and 3C, ran 100 charge–discharge cycles, then took the cells apart. They imaged the electrodes with an electron microscope, measured the crystal spacing with X-ray diffraction, and counted lithium atoms directly with nuclear reaction analysis, where protons hitting lithium-7 give off a gamma ray.

The ideal charging time is simply

t ≈ 60 min ÷ C-rate    (current I = C-rate × rated capacity)

and the crystal spacing comes from Bragg's law with copper Kα X-rays (λ = 1.5406 Å):

d = λ ÷ (2 sin θ),   c = 3·d003 for the layered cathode

The LCO 3C cell used a fixed 60-second constant-voltage hold that left it badly under-charged from the start (6.6 mAh of 45), so the authors treat it as limited by the charging protocol, not as a fair 3C result. The NMC cells above 0.3C were also cycled in a wider 2.7–4.3 V window to speed up ageing. One cell was tested per condition.

How to use it

  • Find the cliff: with NMC811 selected, move the speed slider from 1C to 2C. Charge time halves, but capacity kept drops from 99.7% to 72.3%.
  • Compare chemistries: switch to LCO at 2C. It keeps only 11.1%, and its anode holds about 20 times more lithium than the slow-charged cell.
  • Read the 3C dip correctly: lithium on the anode is lower at 3C than at 2C. That is not because 3C is gentler: the cells could only take up part of their capacity, so less charge moved through them over 100 cycles.
  • Play with Bragg's law: a shift of only 0.06° in the peak angle is a 0.05 Å change in the cathode's lattice, which X-ray diffraction can still resolve.
