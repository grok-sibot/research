Seeing heart arteries clearly through a beating chest

Digital subtraction angiography takes an X-ray before and after dye is injected and subtracts the two, so only the dye-filled vessels remain. That works for the still brain, but the heart beats and the chest breathes between frames, so the subtraction leaves ghosts of the heart's edges. This paper takes both frames with a two-layer detector that records a low-energy and a high-energy image at the same instant, then uses the difference in how iodine and soft tissue absorb the two energies to pull the iodine out on its own.

Yuhang Tan, Jiongtao Zhu, Xin Zhang, Haodi Wu, Xiao Xuan, Mang Feng, Mingzhi Cui, Hairong Zheng, Dong Liang, Yongshuai Ge

Why it matters

Because the heart never sits still, cardiologists can't use ordinary subtraction on coronary arteries, so they inject more dye and tilt the view to dodge the spine and ribs, and small blockages can still hide behind bone. Splitting each frame by X-ray energy removes the moving background without asking the heart to hold still, and in a live pig it brought out small vessels and the faint dye haze in heart muscle (the "myocardial blush") that shows whether blood is actually reaching the tissue.

The paper

Dual-energy cardiac DSA imaging using a dual-layer flat-panel detector: in vivo validation study

Yuhang Tan, Jiongtao Zhu, Xin Zhang, Haodi Wu, Xiao Xuan, Mang Feng, Mingzhi Cui, Hairong Zheng, Dong Liang, Yongshuai Ge (Shenzhen Institutes of Advanced Technology and Wandong Medical). arXiv:2611.08820, November 2026. Read on arXiv · PDF

In plain language: the team subtracts the pre-dye frames from the post-dye frames separately in each energy channel. Bone is lined up by image registration, so what's left in each channel is a little soft tissue that moved, plus the iodine. Because soft tissue and iodine absorb low- and high-energy X-rays in different proportions, two measurements are enough to solve for both at every pixel:

ΔPLE = μT,LE·ΔLT + μI,LE·LI + nLE    ΔPHE = μT,HE·ΔLT + μI,HE·LI + nHE

Solving that 2×2 system directly blows up the noise, because the two energies see the materials only slightly differently. So they minimise a penalised least-squares cost with a total-variation term that favours piecewise-smooth maps, using the ADMM algorithm:

L̂ = argmin ½‖U·L − ΔP‖² + βT·TV(ΔLT) + βI·TV(LI)

Subtracting also cancels most of the scattered X-rays, since scatter is nearly the same before and after the dye: in their Monte Carlo simulations the scatter error dropped by more than 80%. In a phantom, the measured iodine signal was linear in iodine amount (R² = 0.96), and on a clinical DSA system with a live pig the penalised method showed small vessel branches that the direct solution and simple weighted subtraction lost in noise.

This demo runs the paper's residual model and an ADMM solver of the same objective live in your browser, on a made-up 128 × 128 chest. The attenuation values, noise levels, anatomy and motion are illustrative choices for this page, not the paper's calibrated numbers, and the TV step uses Chambolle's exact-TV projection instead of the paper's smoothed TV.

How to use it

  • See why plain DSA fails on the heart: press Still heart, and panel 2 is clean. Then press Beating heart and bright and dark crescents appear at the heart and lung edges, often stronger than the arteries themselves.
  • See what two energies buy you: in panels 3 and 4 the crescents vanish at any motion, because the solve assigns the moved tissue to its own map and keeps only iodine.
  • See why the penalty matters: set smoothing to 0, and panel 4 becomes as noisy as panel 3. Raise it, and the faint blush in the left heart wall appears. Too much smoothing starts to flatten the thinnest artery.
  • Trade dose for frames: lower the dose, then average more pre-dye frames. Averaging only cleans the "before" half of the subtraction, so it helps, but less than more dose would.
