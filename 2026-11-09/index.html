Growing a uniform alloy crystal: why the melt zone's shape matters

To grow a single crystal of the shape-memory alloy Ni–Mn–Ga, a rod is slowly passed through a small molten zone. Manganese prefers to stay in the liquid, so the crystal's composition drifts along its length. The textbook formula for this (Scheil–Pfann) assumes the molten zone never changes size. This paper measures and models a sapphire-crucible version of the method and shows the zone does change, which explains why real crystals depart from the textbook curve.

Denys Musiienko, Taras Kovaliuk, Petr Harcuba, Oleg Heczko, Ross H. Colman

Why it matters

Shape-memory alloys such as Ni–Mn–Ga only work well when every part of the crystal has nearly the same composition, but they melt unevenly, so growers usually end up with crystals that vary from one end to the other. Being able to reconstruct what the molten zone was really doing, from a model checked against a quenched growth front, gives growers a way to tune the process instead of guessing.

The paper

Finite-element reconstruction of molten-zone dynamics and chemical segregation in optical floating-zone grown Ni–Mn–Ga

Denys Musiienko, Taras Kovaliuk, Petr Harcuba, Oleg Heczko, Ross H. Colman. arXiv:2611.08250, submitted 5 Nov 2026. Read on arXiv · PDF

In plain language: the authors grow Ni–Mn–Ga crystals by optical floating-zone growth inside a transparent sapphire crucible, which steadies the molten zone while keeping lamp heating and a view of the melt. They then couple a transient finite-element heat-transfer model (COMSOL) to a mass balance of the molten zone. The model shows that the speed at which the crystal solidifies is not simply the speed the rod is moved, because it varies strongly as the zone geometry evolves. A quenched growth interface, examined by EBSD, supports the calculated shape of the solidification front, and the coupled mass balance reproduces the main features of the measured composition profiles.

The classic baseline is Pfann's zone-melting law for a constant zone length l and segregation coefficient k (the ratio of solid to liquid composition at the front):

Cs(x) / C0 = 1 − (1 − k)·exp(−k·x / l)

A mass balance of the zone, where the feed brings in composition C0, the front takes out k·CL, and the zone length l(x) may change, generalises it:

d(l·CL)/dx = C0 − k·CL,   Cs = k·CL

This page is a simplified one-dimensional illustration of that idea, not the paper's finite-element model: it takes a zone length you choose, rather than computing it from heat flow, and uses a made-up segregation coefficient range, since the paper's fitted values aren't reproduced here. The last zone is frozen without any feed, as in ordinary normal freezing.

How to use it

  • See the textbook curve: choose Ideal constant zone. The crystal starts low in the segregating element, rises, then levels at the rod's own composition, and the final zone shoots up at the end.
  • Let the zone change: try Zone shrinks or Zone widens. The blue curve leaves the green textbook curve in the middle of the crystal, the kind of departure the authors attribute to an evolving zone.
  • Aim for uniformity: raise k towards 1 or lengthen the zone and watch the uniform fraction change. A longer zone takes more of the crystal to settle, so the best choice depends on the alloy.
