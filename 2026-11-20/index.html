Adding up the evidence that the Atlantic's ocean conveyor is slowing

The Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC) carries warm surface water north and cold deep water south, and climate models have long warned that global warming will slow it, possibly past a tipping point. Direct measurements only began in 2004, so whether it is already slowing is still argued about. This review gathers every line of evidence south of 45°N (moorings, ocean reanalyses, temperature, salinity and heat "fingerprints", water age, Gulf Stream position and thousand-year proxy records) and argues that, taken together, they strongly support a slowdown that is already under way. Below, two of the paper's arguments run live: how uncertain clues add up by Bayes' rule, and why a real trend in a short, noisy record can still fail a 95% significance test.

Stefan Rahmstorf, Levke Caesar

Why it matters

The AMOC moves heat, carbon and fresh water around the Atlantic, and a big weakening or collapse could speed up regional sea-level rise, shift rainfall and drought, and cool north-western Europe. This review shows that many independent, imperfect clues all point the same way, so planners should treat an ongoing slowdown as real now, keep and expand the ocean monitoring arrays, and cut emissions quickly rather than wait for certainty.

The paper

The balance of evidence indicates a slowing AMOC

Stefan Rahmstorf (Potsdam Institute for Climate Impact Research; University of Potsdam) and Levke Caesar (Potsdam Institute for Climate Impact Research). arXiv:2611.23429 [physics.ao-ph], submitted 17 November 2026. Read on arXiv · PDF

In plain language: the only continuous measurement of the whole AMOC, the RAPID array at 26°N, has run for about 20 years. The authors' fit to the corrected data (April 2004 to March 2024) gives −0.95 Sv per decade, about 1.9 Sv in total (1 Sv = one million cubic metres of water per second). At that rate the AMOC could fall from its recent average of about 17 Sv to about 10 Sv by 2100, close to where it is likely already past its tipping point. But 20 years is too short to separate climate change from natural ups and downs, so the authors turn to longer, indirect records. They fit straight-line trends to each by ordinary least squares and adjust the p-value for lag-1 autocorrelation of the residuals (this year's wobble partly carries into next year's):

x(t) = a + b·t + ε(t),    total change = b × (years covered),    p adjusted for r1 = corr(εt, εt−1)

Their key point is about combining evidence. A trend that misses the 95% threshold is weaker evidence for slowing, not evidence against it. And several uncertain clues multiply up by Bayes' rule:

P(S | E) = P(E | S)·P(S) / [ P(E | S)·P(S) + P(E | not S)·(1 − P(S)) ]

Their worked example: start sceptical at 50:50. The North Atlantic "cold blob" would be seen 75% of the time if the AMOC slowed, but still 33% of the time if it did not, so the odds become 69%, "likely" in IPCC terms. Add the South Atlantic salinity pile-up (80% vs 20%) and the probability goes past 90%, "very likely".

Other lines in the review: four mooring arrays from 16.5°N to 42.5°N show the deep western overturning weakening by 2.6 ± 0.7 Sv per decade at 26°N over 2004–2023; ocean reanalyses give 1.23 ± 0.57 Sv per decade and a 15% drop in heat transport at 26°N over three decades; a water-mass transformation study finds 13% ± 2% (~2.4 Sv) decline since 1980; North Atlantic water is "generally aging"; the Gulf Stream has shifted 55 km north over 1993–2024; and proxy records show the weakest AMOC in at least a millennium. Studies finding no trend are mostly north of 45°N, where the flow is more complex. The authors call for a sustained early-warning monitoring system, planning for possible further weakening, and rapid emission cuts.

How to use it

  • Press "Paper's example": with a 50% prior, the cold blob alone lifts the probability to 69%, and adding the salinity pile-up takes it just past 90%, matching the paper.
  • Try "Strong sceptic": even starting at 10%, the same two clues multiply the odds by about nine; turn on clue 3 and watch how fast belief climbs. The right-hand curve shows the result for every possible starting belief at once.
  • Slide "Overlap" up: clues that share a cause (the paper notes the northern and southern salinity signals are not fully independent) count for less. At 100% overlap only the first clue counts.
  • Set a clue's two chances equal: evidence that is just as likely either way changes nothing. Make "if not slowed" bigger than "if slowed" and it lowers belief, which is how evidence of a strengthening AMOC would count.
  • In Try it 2, press "RAPID-like" and then "New noise" several times: the slowing is real every time, yet the p-value often lands above 0.05. Then stretch the record length and watch the catch rate rise. This is why the paper says a non-significant trend is not evidence against slowing.
