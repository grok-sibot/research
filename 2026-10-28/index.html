The ice shelf that can’t switch back

Floating Antarctic ice shelves are melted from below by the ocean. Sea ice freezing in open-water patches (polynyas) at the ice front makes surface water salty and heavy, and that cold water sinks and shields the cavity. This study shows that if sea-ice formation weakens enough to let warm deep water in, the high-melt state can lock itself in.

Louis Saddier, Corentin Herbert, Christopher Y. S. Bull & Louis-Alexandre Couston

Why it matters

Ice shelves hold back the grounded Antarctic ice that raises sea level when it slides into the ocean, and regional models suggest cavities such as Filchner-Ronne and Amery could flip from cold, slow-melting to warm, fast-melting this century. This study shows that flip can be irreversible: once weaker sea-ice formation lets warm water in, returning to the original sea-ice conditions does not switch it back, and the depth of the warm layer at the ice front can serve as a warning sign.

In plain language: beneath every ice shelf is a cavity of seawater. Some cavities are filled with water at the surface freezing point (about −1.9 °C) and melt slowly. Others are flooded by Circumpolar Deep Water at about +1 °C and melt fast. Which one you get depends on the polynya in front: when lots of sea ice forms there, the salt it squeezes out makes the surface water dense enough to sink all the way down (deep convection), mixing away the warm layer.

The authors ran a 3D ocean model of an idealised 100 km ice shelf and slowly turned the polynya’s salt flux FS up, then down, in 10-year steps over 160 years. Going up, the cavity stays warm until a high threshold; going down, it stays cold until a much lower one. In between, both states are stable (bistability), so the history decides. The reason is a feedback: warm-mode meltwater is light and fresh, a gyre at the ice front keeps it trapped over the polynya, and that fresh lid stops convection. The overlap widens with warmer offshore water and shrinks with stronger salinity layering; with free-slip walls (no gyre) the switch becomes gradual and fully reversible.

Other results: warm-mode melt fits a power law with exponent 2.06 (for thermal driving above 2 °C), cold-mode melt 1.05. The cold-to-warm threshold is set mainly by the offshore density layering, while the warm-to-cold threshold is set by how much meltwater the warm cavity makes. Only relative salinity matters: changing surface or bottom salinity by the same amount gives identical melt. The thermocline depth under the polynya tracks the regime and is proposed as an indicator.

Limits: this is an idealised box (flat 600 m seabed, linear ice base, prescribed far-field ocean, sea ice represented only as a salt flux) at 2 km resolution, which only partly resolves the meltwater boundary current. The thresholds depend on model choices like viscosity and side-wall friction, which may explain why earlier realistic studies disagree about reversibility. The demo’s numbers are illustrative, not the paper’s exact values.

The maths

Thermal driving uses the freezing point at the deepest ice draft, Tb(H) = −2.33 °C. The paper’s melt scaling laws, normalised in the demo so the TEMP1 warm state at FS = 0 is 1, with small FS trends matching Fig. 7 (warm melt falls as the thermocline deepens, cold melt rises as the front gyre strengthens):

ΔT = T0 − Tb(H) mwarm ∝ ΔT² mcold ∝ ΔT

The buoyancy budget under the polynya (Equations 2–4): the regime flips when the buoyancy removed by brine balances the buoyancy added by meltwater, with polynya area 𝒜p and ice-shelf area 𝒜c:

Because the warm state makes far more meltwater than the cold one, the budget predicts two different thresholds, and the gap between them is the bistable range. Rescaled with Equation 5, all bistable runs collapse onto one curve, but warm-to-cold happens at fS ≈ 0.4 rather than the predicted 1, because some meltwater escapes the polynya.

Demo rules (toy calibration): FSwarm→cold = 0.045 × ΔT / 3.33 (rises with the meltwater the warm state makes), and FScold→warm = 0.032 × exp[1.9 (ΔS − 0.5)] (rises with offshore layering, independent of T0). If the second is at least the first, or the free-slip option is chosen, there is no overlap and the demo blends the two branches smoothly around the midpoint.

How to use it

  • Run the experiment: press the blue button. FS climbs from 0 to 0.075 and back, like the paper’s two 160-year runs. Watch the purple dot stay on the red warm branch long past the point where it later returns on the blue cold branch.
  • Feel the memory: press “Start warm”, drag FS to the middle of the purple zone, then press “Start cold” at the same setting. Same forcing, very different melt.
  • The irreversible step: at the default settings press “Start cold” with FS at 0.040, drag it down until the cavity flips warm (below 0.032), then drag it back to 0.040. It stays warm: you have to push past 0.045 to recover the cold state.
  • Warmer ocean: raise T0 to 2 °C and the overlap widens; drop it to 0 °C or below and the switch becomes reversible, as in the paper.
  • Layering and circulation: raise ΔS to 0.7 or pick free-slip and the overlap disappears.
