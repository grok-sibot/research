A short equation for monsoon rain, found by AI

How hard it rains in the South Asian monsoon depends on how warm, moist and unstable the lowest few kilometres of air are, but weather and climate models squeeze that whole column into a rough rule. This paper lets neural networks learn which heights matter, then uses symbolic regression to boil what they learned down to a one-line formula. The formula says moisture and instability compete rather than add up: rain is set by whichever of the two is stronger.

Savannah L. Ferretti, Jane W. Baldwin, Michael S. Pritchard, Tom Beucler

Why it matters

More than a billion people depend on monsoon rain, and climate models still struggle to get it right because rain forms at scales far smaller than their grid boxes and has to be estimated from simple rules. A readable formula that explains 1.5 times as much rainfall variation as the standard buoyancy rule, and keeps about 85% of a neural network's skill with about a thousand times fewer parameters, is something modellers can check against physics and build into the rain schemes of weather and climate models.

The paper

Discovering Equations for the Thermodynamics of South Asian Monsoon Precipitation

Savannah L. Ferretti, Jane W. Baldwin, Michael S. Pritchard, Tom Beucler (University of California Irvine, NVIDIA and University of Lausanne). arXiv:2611.14401, November 2026. Read on arXiv · PDF

In plain language: the authors trained on 21 summers (June to August) of ERA5 reanalysis over South Asia, predicting 3-hourly rainfall. A neural network first learned one vertical weighting curve for each of three profiles: relative humidity (RH, weighted most near 500 hPa), equivalent potential temperature θe (how much energy a rising air parcel carries, weighted most at the surface) and saturation equivalent potential temperature θe* (how hard the surrounding air is to rise through, weighted most around 750 hPa). Each profile becomes one number, written with a hat. Symbolic regression then searched for the simplest formula linking those numbers to rain and found:

P = ( exp(λ · max(M, I)3) − 1 )+
M = κ (RĤ − μRH),   I = θ̂e − γ θ̂e* − Θc

with λ = 0.001137 K−3, κ = 0.4141 K per %, γ = 1.045 and Θc = −25.99 K (the paper's Table S2), and P in mm per 3 hours. M is a moisture pathway and I an instability pathway, both in kelvin. Because of the max, extra instability does nothing while the air is moisture-limited, and the other way round. The baseline it beats fits rain to the lower-tropospheric buoyancy BL of Ahmed and Neelin, which adds moisture and instability together:

P = ( exp(λB (BL − Bc)3 + β) − 1 )+,   λB = 1687 s6/m3, Bc = −0.09673 m/s², β = 0.06791

The constants and formulas are the paper's. It does not list the training mean μRH or typical values of the weighted features, so the humidity slider here is the departure from that mean, and the θ̂e and θ̂e* slider ranges are this page's assumption (typical monsoon values in kelvin). Treat the numbers as an illustration of the formula's shape, not a forecast.

How to use it

  • Find the competition: press Hot, energetic surface air, then drag the humidity slider. The rain stays flat until M overtakes I, because the max ignores the weaker pathway.
  • Follow the dashed line: on the map, rain contours are square corners along M = I. A rule that added the two would give diagonal contours instead.
  • Raise θ̂e* to suppress rain: warmer, harder-to-penetrate air around 750 hPa lowers I, as convective physics expects. The paper checks that the equation always obeys these three signs: more humidity and more parcel energy never reduce rain, and more environmental θe* never increases it.
  • Compare with the baseline: slide BL past the threshold Bc to see the cubic-exponential take-off the buoyancy fit learned. It is one number, so it cannot tell a moisture-limited sky from an instability-limited one.
