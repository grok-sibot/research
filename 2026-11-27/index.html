How often and how long to feed or pump: a maths model of milk supply

Taking milk out of the breast tells the breast to keep making it. That feedback is why "feed often, empty well" is standard lactation advice, but the advice has stayed qualitative. Mathematicians at the University of Vienna have now written that feedback as a small dynamical system: milk builds up between sessions, each session removes some of it, and the amount removed boosts the production rate for later. Stepping this model forward session by session predicts when a schedule keeps supply going, when supply quietly dies out, and which schedule gives the most milk per day. The demo below runs the paper's model live.

Angelika Manhart, Tobias Müller

Why it matters

Trouble building or keeping up milk supply is common and worrying for breastfeeding and pumping parents, especially those of premature babies, yet the guidance on how often and how long to feed or pump is mostly rules of thumb. This model turns the "removal drives production" feedback into testable maths, predicting a critical gap between sessions beyond which supply dies out whatever you do, and showing that emptying the breast every session does not always give the most milk per day.

The paper

Milk-removal feedback shapes lactation sustainability and daily output in a model of human lactation

Angelika Manhart and Tobias Müller (Faculty of Mathematics, University of Vienna, Austria). arXiv:2611.33997 [q-bio.QM], submitted 25 November 2026. Read on arXiv · PDF · Authors' code and explorer

In plain language: the model tracks two numbers for one breast: how much milk is stored, m (ml), and how fast it is being made, p (ml per hour). Between sessions milk builds up at rate p and is slowly reabsorbed, while the production rate fades away unless something keeps it up. Each feed or pumping session removes some milk, and removing milk gives the production rate a boost. More milk removed gives a bigger boost, up to a ceiling. The key point is that this is a loop. If supply drops, less milk comes out, so the next boost is smaller, and supply drops further. The authors prove when that downward spiral wins and when supply settles at a steady level.

Between sessions (wait T hours) the model is two simple equations, solved exactly:

dm/dt = p − δm,    dp/dt = −γp   ⇒   x = m·e−δT + p·G(T),   G(T) = (e−γT − e−δT)/(δ − γ)

At a session, an amount M is removed from the x ml available and production jumps by a stimulation a(M):

mn = xn − Mn,    pn = e−γT pn−1 + a(Mn)

Set-duration sessions remove M = x·min(τ/τemp, 1), where τemp is the time it takes to empty the breast. Set-target sessions remove M = min(Mtar, x). The boost a(M) is either Michaelis–Menten, amaxM/(M+μ), where small removals work best per ml, or Hill, amaxM²/(M²+μ²), where small removals barely count. Lactation can only last if the boost per ml, R(M) = a(M)/M, can beat the fade over one cycle, Γ(T) = (1 − e−γT)/G(T). Γ grows with the wait, so there is a critical wait Tcrit, with Γ(Tcrit) = max R. Wait longer than that between sessions and supply always dies out. Daily output in the steady state is Mday = 24·M*/(T + τ*).

Try it: design a feeding or pumping schedule

Defaults are the paper's order-of-magnitude estimates for one breast in established lactation. Production fades over about 48 h without stimulation (γ = δ = 1/48 h⁻¹). An efficient pump empties the breast in about 18 min. Exclusive breastfeeding gives about 18 ml/h per breast. The stimulation parameters are calibrated so that a feed every 3 h that empties the breast is a steady state. These numbers are not fitted to patient data, so treat the outputs as illustrations, not advice.

What the paper finds

  • A critical wait. When the boost depends on how much milk comes out, a falling supply means less milk out, which means a weaker boost, so the fall feeds itself. For every wait longer than Tcrit, the only long-run state is zero production, however long or hard each session is. With the paper's estimates, Tcrit is roughly one to two days.
  • Two kinds of steady supply. For waits shorter than Tcrit, supply settles either to a "target met" state, where the session goal is reached with milk left behind, or to an "emptying" state, where every session drains the breast. The largest goal that can be met every time, Memp(T), shrinks as the wait grows.
  • A possible establishment threshold. If small removals barely stimulate (the Hill response), supply can be bistable. The same schedule keeps a well-established supply going but lets a weak one die out. This looks like the clinical idea that lactation has to be established first, though the model does not prove that is the cause.
  • Emptying is not always best. A longer session gets more milk each time but leaves less time for more sessions. With the paper's estimates, for waits shorter than about 2–3 h, stopping before the breast is empty gives more milk per day.
  • Time-based boosts behave differently. If stimulation depends on session length instead of amount removed, continued sessions can never drive supply all the way to zero. With a Hill response and a set target, supply can even oscillate from session to session, but only for waits over about 53 h.

This demo implements the amount-dependent model (Sec. 3.1–3.2 and 4.2 of the paper). The duration-dependent variants are in the authors' own interactive explorer, linked above.

How to use it

  • Start at the defaults: a session every 3 h, 18 min long, which just empties the breast. Supply holds at about 18 ml/h and gives about 375 ml a day from one breast, so roughly 750 ml from two. That matches the 700–800 ml a day that exclusively breastfed babies typically drink.
  • Drag Wait between sessions to the right. Daily output falls, and once you pass the red critical-wait line (about 40 h here) the 10-day trace shows production sliding towards zero. In the left chart, the red Γ(T) line has risen above the whole purple boost curve.
  • Set the wait to about 1 h and look at the daily-output chart. Its peak sits to the left of the grey "emptied" line, so shorter sessions that leave some milk behind give more milk per day. On the schedule map, the green best-length curve drops below the emptying time for short waits.
  • Switch to small amounts barely count. With the paper's Hill calibration, starting at 18 ml/h with a 1–2 h wait now dies out, while a 3 h wait from the same start climbs to a much larger supply. Raise Production rate at the start above about 35 ml/h and a 1.5 h wait works again. That is bistability: the outcome depends on where you start. The purple curve now crosses the red line twice. The left crossing is the threshold Msep, and the right one is Memp.
  • Switch to when a target amount is out to model a baby who takes a set amount or a pumping goal. A target above Memp cannot be met at every session, so each session drains the breast.

Demo maths: the session-to-session map above is iterated exactly, with no time-stepping error, from m₀ = 0 and your starting production. The long-run numbers average the last 50 sessions after about 1,500 h of simulated time, using Mday = 24·ΣM/Σ(T + τ). In set-target mode the session length is τ = τemp·Mtar/x, or τemp if the breast is drained. Tcrit solves Γ(T) = max R by bisection. Default stimulation follows the paper's Sec. 6.1 estimate, a(51 ml) ≈ 1.1 ml/h with μ = 100 ml. That gives amax = 3.26 ml/h for Michaelis–Menten (max R ≈ 0.032 h⁻¹) and 5.33 ml/h for Hill (max R ≈ 0.027 h⁻¹). Under the Hill calibration, the 3-hourly 51 ml state sits exactly on the unstable threshold, which is why that response jumps to a larger supply. This is a model study with no clinical data, and it is not breastfeeding or pumping advice.
