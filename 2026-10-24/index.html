Cancer-scan copper from plain zinc

Copper-64 lights up tumours on PET scans and can also treat them, but today it is made from a rare, enriched nickel that costs about US$50,000 a gram. This team shone a laser-driven fusion neutron source on ordinary zinc for one hour. The only radioactive copper they could find in it afterwards was copper-64.

Jeremy M. C. Brown, Mantas Grigalavicius, Tibor Gilinger, Adam Kovacs, Parvin Varmazyar, Hsin-Hui Huang, Saulius Juodkazis, Mikas Vengris, Darius Gailevicius & Karoly Osvay

Why it matters

Copper-64 can both image and treat prostate, brain and skin cancers, but it is made today from enriched nickel-64 at about US$50,000 per gram, which keeps it out of most clinics. This experiment made it instead from natural zinc at about US$24 per gram, roughly 2,000 times cheaper, and found no other radioactive copper, giving a purity of at least 99.62% and 99.97% after the paper’s detection-limit correction, above the 99.95% ultra-high-purity bar.

The paper

In plain language: when a fast neutron hits a zinc-64 nucleus, it can knock out a proton and turn the zinc into copper-64 (the 64Zn(n,p)64Cu reaction). Natural zinc also contains zinc-66, -67, -68 and -70, which can turn into unwanted copper-66, -67 and -68. Copper-67 is the real problem, because its 61.8-hour half-life is longer than copper-64’s 12.7 hours and it cannot be removed chemically. Reactor neutrons make too much of it.

The team used the LEIA beamline at the ELI-ALPS laser facility in Hungary. A 1 kHz laser fires deuterons from a sheet of heavy water into a second heavy-water layer, where deuterium–deuterium fusion releases neutrons with a narrow spread of energies around 3.1 ± 0.5 MeV, about 2 × 10⁷ per second. At that energy the reactions make copper-64 about 682 times faster than copper-67. A 5 mm zinc pellet was irradiated for one hour, then measured with a germanium gamma-ray detector for 6 hours in every 12 over the next 72 hours. Only copper-64’s gamma lines appeared, decaying with its 12.7-hour half-life. Copper-66 and -68 had died away during the 0.67 hours it took to move the sample, and copper-67 stayed below the detection limit.

The maths

Each copper isotope i is made at a constant rate Ri = fi R while the neutrons are on, where fi is its fractional total cross-section (Table II), and decays with λi = ln 2 / T½,i. For an irradiation of length tirr, the activity A = λN is:

during: Ai(t) = Ri (1 − e−λit) after (τ = t − tirr): Ai = Ri (1 − e−λitirr) e−λiτ

Copper-68 is fed through the 3.75-minute isomer ⁶⁸ᵐCu, so it uses the two-member Bateman solution for a parent P feeding a daughter D:

ND(τ) = ND(0) e−λDτ + NP(0) λP/(λD − λP) · (e−λPτ − e−λDτ)

Radioisotopic purity is the copper-64 share of all copper activity. The free fit to the six measured points is a straight line through ln A versus time, and its slope gives T½ = −ln 2 / slope.

purity = A64 / (A64 + A66 + A67 + A68 + A68m) f = 0.9984, 1.448×10⁻⁴, 1.465×10⁻³, 1.504×10⁻⁸

Half-lives used: ⁶⁴Cu 12.70 h, ⁶⁶Cu 0.08533 h, ⁶⁷Cu 61.83 h, ⁶⁸Cu 0.008583 h, ⁶⁸ᵐCu 0.0625 h. Simplifications: all mass-68 production is routed through ⁶⁸ᵐCu, which all becomes ⁶⁸Cu; the 21-second ⁶⁷Ni route to ⁶⁷Cu is treated as direct; there is no burn-up and no change in neutron output. The absolute scale R is fitted with T½ fixed at 12.7 h to Table I, giving about 21.4 Bq of copper-64 at the end of the 1 h run.

How to use it

  • Irradiation time: longer runs make more copper-64, which saturates after a few half-lives. Copper-67 keeps building for longer because it lives longer, so the purity window shrinks. Try 1 h and then 12 h.
  • Wait after irradiation: the first few minutes are dominated by short-lived copper-66. Once it has decayed, purity peaks. After that, the longer-lived copper-67 slowly gains on copper-64 until purity drops below 99.95%. The paper’s sample was first measured at 0.67 h.
  • Source strength and zinc mass: these scale the amount of copper-64 but not its purity. The experiment made about 20 Bq. A single PET dose is millions of times more, which is why the authors see the next step as bright deuteron accelerators (an earlier paper from the same lab targets 10¹⁰ n/s), with clinical-scale production in about 5 years.
  • Measured decay: the right-hand plot shows that the six real measurements fall on copper-64’s 12.7-hour decay, which is how the team knew the 0.511 MeV signal came only from copper-64.
