Beyond special quasirandom structures: free energies from energy cumulants

Yann L. Müller and Anirudh Raju Natarajan — EPFL, Laboratory of materials design and simulation (MADES)

arXiv:2610.11882 (submitted 8 Oct 2026, cond-mat.mtrl-sci) · arxiv.org/abs/2610.11882 · PDF

Why it matters

Designing better battery cathodes and high-temperature alloys means deciding which disordered mixtures of elements can actually be made, and today that needs expensive simulations for every candidate. This method gets a far more accurate stability estimate from a few hundred cheap energy evaluations, so researchers can screen thousands of compositions before ever entering a lab.

The idea in plain language

A disordered material (say, lithium and metal atoms scattered randomly over a crystal lattice) can be arranged in astronomically many ways, each with a slightly different energy. The standard shortcut, a “special quasirandom structure”, takes the average energy of these arrangements and adds the ideal entropy of mixing. The authors show this is just the first term of an exact series in the cumulants of the energy distribution: the mean, then the variance (how spread out the energies are), then the skew, and so on.

Keeping the variance lets the model capture that, on cooling, atoms prefer low-energy arrangements (short-range order). In the paper’s benchmark on 502 equiatomic alloys of a nine-element refractory system, keeping the variance cut free-energy errors by roughly an order of magnitude. They also screened Li-excess rocksalt oxide cathodes, ranking candidates for the Li₄ clusters that let lithium move, and note the screen is a ranking, not a quantitative prediction.

How to use it

  1. Pick a disordered composition and generate a few hundred random atomic arrangements on the lattice (the paper uses random decorations of supercells, not Monte Carlo).
  2. Evaluate their energies with a fast model, for example a foundation machine-learned potential (the paper uses GRACE).
  3. Compute the energy mean, variance and skew (cumulants). Plug them into the truncated or binomial free-energy formulas above; no further sampling is needed.
  4. Compare the resulting disordered-phase free energy with competing ordered phases to estimate where the disordered phase becomes stable, then send only the best candidates to heavier simulation.

Caveats from the paper: truncated series lose validity at low temperature; accuracy is inherited from the underlying energy model; free energies are configurational only (no vibrations); the screening estimate over-predicts Li₄-cluster probability versus Monte Carlo, so near-threshold candidates need verification.
