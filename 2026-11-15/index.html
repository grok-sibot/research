A safe blood-pressure target for each surgery patient

Under general anaesthesia blood pressure often sags, and anaesthetists give the drug norepinephrine to lift it. The same dose lifts one patient a lot and another barely at all. This paper learns each patient's own response from about ten minutes of routine readings, works out how unsure that fit still is, and then picks the lowest pressure target, with a bolus and a steady drip, whose predicted chance of any reading below 65 mmHg stays under a chosen limit. If no target is safe enough, it says so and leaves the decision to the clinician.

Simon Buré, Jona Joachim, Florent Di Meglio

Why it matters

Low blood pressure is common during surgery, and even short dips of the mean arterial pressure below 65 mmHg are linked to kidney injury, heart injury and death, yet norepinephrine is still adjusted by hand although patients respond very differently to the same dose. This method fits each patient's response from about ten minutes of routine readings and chooses the lowest target it can reach safely, or hands the decision back: in 4,096 virtual patients, a fixed 70 mmHg target left 25% with a reading below 65 mmHg, while the personalised rule treated 74% of patients and only 6.9% of them dipped below.

The paper

A risk-aware individualized blood pressure target to avoid hypotension in surgery

Simon Buré, Jona Joachim, Florent Di Meglio (Centre Automatique et Systèmes, Mines Paris – PSL; Université Paris Cité; St-Louis-Lariboisière-Fernand Widal University Hospitals, Paris). arXiv:2611.16757 [q-bio.QM], 12 November 2026. Read on arXiv · PDF · Code

In plain language: the drug moves through three "compartments" (a depot near the drip, the blood, and the tissues), and the blood pressure rises with the amount in the blood. The team simplified a published twelve-parameter model to seven parameters that can be estimated from blood pressure readings alone, with no blood samples. With the infusion rate u (nmol/s):

Ȧd = u − kaAd,   Ȧc = kaAd − (k12+kel)Ac + k21Ap,   Ȧp = k12Ac − k21Ap
MAP = h(Ac) = Emax − a ÷ (b + Ac)

A patient is fitted by maximum a posteriori least squares around the population model. Linearising the model around that fit gives a Gaussian spread of the patient's parameters, P = (SᵀS/σ² + I)⁻¹, which turns into a forecast of every future reading. The risk of a target y is the chance that at least one reading in the window after the rise falls under 65 mmHg, and the chosen target is the smallest one under the limit α:

R(y) = 1 − Eδη[ ∏j Φ((ŷj + [S′δη]j − 65) ÷ σ) ],    y* = min{ y ∈ [65, F̂(umax)] : R(y) ≤ α }

The dose for a target is a steady drip ū = kel·h⁻¹(y) plus the smallest 10-second bolus that gets the pressure to the target within the rise time.

Try it: treat a virtual patient

Each virtual patient starts with low blood pressure. For the first minutes everyone gets the same collection drip (0.36 nmol/s for 2.5 min, then 0.18 nmol/s), as in the paper. At the decision time the model is fitted to the noisy readings, and the rule picks a target and a dose.

The single 89 mmHg target meets 5% but needs nearly three times the drug, and could only be found by trying every target on the whole cohort. The rule slightly overshoots its promised level (6.9% against 5%); most of those low readings are monitor noise, and the true pressure went below 65 mmHg for about one patient in a thousand. On 31 real surgery records, the forecast was well calibrated after 5–15 minutes of data but became overconfident with longer records.

How to use it

  • Meet a patient: press New virtual patient. Grey dots are the noisy readings during the collection drip; the blue line is the model fitted to them.
  • Read the decision: after the dashed line the blue band is the forecast under the recommended dose. The rule pushes the target up until the band's chance of touching 65 mmHg falls below α. If even the highest allowed drip cannot get there, the patient is refused and the decision goes back to the clinician (rare in this simplified demo, but about a quarter of patients in the paper at α = 5%).
  • Trade safety for drug: move α from 10% down to 1%. The risk curve's crossing point moves right, so the target and the dose go up.
  • Wait for more data: lengthen the collection from 5 to 20 minutes. The fit becomes surer, so for many patients the target can come down a few mmHg.
  • Turn up the monitor noise: with σ = 4 mmHg any of the dozens of readings in the window can dip low by chance, so the target rises by several mmHg. In the paper too, most low readings under y* were noise rather than true low pressure.
  • Compare with a fixed target: the last stat applies the fitted 70 mmHg dose to the same true patient. It often dips below 65 mmHg, because 70 leaves no room for the fit's uncertainty.
