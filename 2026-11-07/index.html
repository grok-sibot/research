Life expectancy as a mix of simpler lives

The Siler model describes mortality from birth to old age as three risks stacked together — falling infant/child risk, a flat background, and rising senescence. Its remaining life expectancy has no ordinary closed form. This paper shows it is exactly a Poisson-weighted mix of simpler Gompertz–Makeham life expectancies, with a closed-form bound on how many years early-life mortality still steals.

Silvio C. Patricio

Why it matters

Public-health and demography teams can now compute — and bound — how many years of remaining life expectancy infant and child mortality still steal, without opaque numerical integration as the only answer. Fitted to Swedish female mortality, that loss at birth collapsed from 4.28 years in 1900 to 0.49 in 1950 and 0.004 in 2020, explaining about 15% of the 27.9-year gain in life expectancy at birth.

The paper

An Exact Poisson Mixture Representation of Siler Remaining Life Expectancy

Silvio C. Patricio (Interdisciplinary Center on Population Dynamics, University of Southern Denmark). arXiv:2611.06376 [stat.AP], submitted 4 Nov 2026. Read on arXiv · PDF

In plain language: Siler’s hazard is the sum of three pieces

μ(t) = a1·e−b1·t + a2 + a3·eb3·t

At age x, the remaining immaturity mass Ax = a1·e−b1·x / b1 is the mean of a Poisson random variable Kx. Corollary 1 says remaining life expectancy is the Poisson mixture

ex = E[ eGMx(a2 + b1·Kx) ] = Σn wn(x)·Qn(x)

where wn = e−Ax·Axn / n! and each Qn is Gompertz–Makeham remaining LE with background c = a2 + n·b1 and the same senescence hazard. The leading term e−Ax·eGMx(a2) is the life expectancy with immaturity removed, times the chance of escaping it. The loss Δx = eGMx(a2) − ex is years stolen by immaturity risk. Proposition 2 bounds the truncation error of the partial sum by Pr(Kx > m) / (a2 + (m+1)·b1).

  • Swedish female fits (HMD, 1900–2020). Loss at birth Δ0 fell from 4.28 years (1900) to 0.49 (1950) and 0.004 (2020). Over that span e0 rose 27.9 years and eGM0(a2) rose 23.6 years, so immaturity decline accounts for 15% of the gain.
  • Tight bound. In every year, Δ0 sits between 98.6% and 99.99% of its upper bound (1 − e−A0)·eGM0(a2), differing by at most 0.060 years.
  • Series accuracy. Stopping when the Prop. 2 bound is below 10−12 years, the series and adaptive quadrature of the survival integral differ by at most 1.08×10−12 years on the checked years/ages.

Limits noted in the paper: the application concerns the Siler series, not how well Siler fits HMD tables (the fitted model overstates e0 by up to 2.6 years before 1940 and understates e60 by about 2–3%); Kx is an auxiliary expansion device, not a count of deaths; year-by-year MLE parameter tables are not published in the paper — the demo presets below are illustrative schedules in the same qualitative regime.

How to use it

  • Match the paper’s story: tap 1900-like, then 2020-like, with age at 0. Watch Δ0 drop from roughly four years toward near zero — the qualitative collapse the Swedish fits quantify as 4.28 → 0.004 years.
  • Raise age: drag age upward. Ax decays at rate b1, the Poisson mass piles onto n=0, and ex approaches the Gompertz–Makeham curve.
  • Turn immaturity up: increase a1 (or lower b1). Ax and Pr(immaturity death) rise, more mixture terms matter, and the loss Δx grows toward its upper bound (1−e−Ax)·eGM.
  • Read the bound: the truncation-bound stat is Prop. 2’s analytic ceiling on the omitted Poisson tail, in years — the reason the series is practical for demography software.
