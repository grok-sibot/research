How good is a forecast really? Fixing the small-ensemble penalty

Seasonal and weather forecasts are run as ensembles: many slightly different runs whose average is compared with what really happened. With only a handful of runs, random noise in that average makes the forecast look less skilful than it is. This ECMWF note gathers the fix into one estimator, r∞, that predicts the skill of an infinitely large ensemble, and argues that when it comes out impossible (below −1, above 1, or undefined), that is a useful warning rather than something to hide.

Christopher David Roberts

Why it matters

Forecast centres decide which models to trust, and how many ensemble members to pay for, by comparing correlation skill, yet the plain correlation unfairly punishes small ensembles and so muddies those comparisons. Using one unbiased signal estimate for both skill and the “ratio of predictable components” lets forecasters judge models fairly, regardless of ensemble size, and say honestly when the data are too few to tell whether a real signal exists.

The paper

Estimating ensemble-mean anomaly correlations and the ratio of predictable components using an unbiased estimate of the predictable signal variance

Christopher David Roberts (ECMWF, Reading, UK). arXiv:2611.07878 [physics.ao-ph], submitted 5 Nov 2026. Read on arXiv · PDF

In plain language: each forecast member is a shared predictable signal plus its own random noise, zk,j = sj + nk,j, for member k and case j (for example, one start date). Averaging N members leaves some noise behind, so the ensemble mean’s variance is too big and the usual correlation with observations, r, is pulled towards zero. Because noise in different members doesn’t correlate, the true signal variance can be recovered without bias (Eq. 8):

E[s²] = ( N·E[⟨z⟩N²] − E[zk²] ) / (N − 1)

Swapping it into the denominator of the correlation gives the ensemble-size-adjusted skill (Eq. 11), which targets the correlation between the predictable signal and the truth:

r∞ = E[⟨z⟩N·zT] / √( E[s²]·E[zT²] )

The same signal variance gives the model-to-model correlation of an infinite ensemble, rmm,∞ = √(E[s²]/E[zk²]), and the size-free ratio of predictable components RPC∞ = |r∞| / rmm,∞ (Eqs. 18–19). RPC above 1 means the real world looks more predictable than the model’s own members, the so-called signal-to-noise paradox. The older variance-based estimates are biased low by an exact factor (Eqs. 20–21):

RPCE14 = γN·RPC∞,   RPCH22 = √γN·RPC∞,   γN = N·rmm,∞² / (1 + (N−1)·rmm,∞²)

The author is clear that this is a technical note that brings together established results (Murphy 1990; Rowell et al. 1995; the SpecsVerification EnsCorr routine), not a brand-new method. What’s new is the emphasis on reporting inadmissible values instead of clipping them, and applying the unbiased signal variance consistently to both parts of the RPC. The paper’s examples use idealised Gaussian data, as this demo does.

How to use it

  • See the penalty: start with Reliable, strong signal. The orange band (plain r) rises with N, so a 3-member ensemble looks worse than a 50-member one even though the model is identical. The blue band (r∞) stays flat on the green true value.
  • Make the model too noisy: pick Overdispersive, weak signal, or raise α above β. Plain r sinks further, but r∞ still recovers ρ. True RPC climbs above 1, the signal-to-noise paradox, and the variance-based RPCs stay stuck below it, following the dashed Eq. 20 curves.
  • Run out of data: pick Reliable, small sample, weak signal or slide M down. The grey line shows how often r∞ becomes undefined or falls outside [−1, 1]. The paper’s point is to report that rate as a sign the signal can’t be pinned down yet, not to clip it away.
  • Check one experiment: move the N slider to see a single draw’s numbers and scatter plot. Press New random draw to see how much they jump around.
