El Niño as a clock nudged by the seasons

The El Niño–Southern Oscillation swings the tropical Pacific between warm El Niño and cool La Niña every two to seven years, and the calendar year keeps pushing it around. This paper turns 50 years of sea-surface temperatures into two clock hands, one for the year and one for ENSO, and learns from the data how the year speeds up or slows down the ENSO hand. The fitted equations predict how long an El Niño or La Niña lasts depending on the month it starts, and show that ENSO tends to linger near a rhythm of one cycle every three years.

Takahiro Arai, Yoji Kawamura

Why it matters

El Niño and La Niña shift rainfall, drought, heatwaves and cyclone risk around the world, so forecasters need to know not just whether an event is coming but how long it will last once it starts. Using two equations learned from 50 years of ocean temperatures, this paper shows that an event's expected length depends on the month it begins (El Niño lasts about 20 months if it starts in May–July but about 17 if it starts in November–February), and that ENSO statistically lingers near one cycle every three years, a simple, testable rule for forecasts and climate models.

The paper

Data-driven Reconstruction of the Phase Dynamics for the El Niño-Southern Oscillation Reveals Seasonality and Statistical Synchronization with the Annual Cycle

Takahiro Arai, Yoji Kawamura (Center for Mathematical Science and Advanced Technology, Japan Agency for Marine-Earth Science and Technology (JAMSTEC), Yokohama). arXiv:2611.14868 [physics.geo-ph; also nlin.AO, physics.ao-ph], 11 November 2026. Read on arXiv · PDF

In plain language: a Koopman (dynamic-mode) analysis of monthly ERSSTv5 sea-surface temperatures (1970–2025) finds two rhythms, the annual cycle (0.991 cycles per year) and the ENSO cycle (0.244 cycles per year, about four years). Each is reduced to an angle: θa for the time of year and θe for the ENSO stage (El Niño around θe = 0, La Niña around π). Bayesian regression then fits how fast each angle turns:

θ̇a = Qa(θa, θe) + ηa(t),    θ̇e = Qe(θa, θe) + ηe(t)

where Q are two-dimensional Fourier series and η is white noise. Qa is almost exactly one cycle per year, while Qe clearly depends on the month: the year modulates ENSO's speed. Averaging over fast terms leaves equations that depend only on the phase difference ψmn = θa − (m/n)θe; for the 3 : 1 ratio, ψ = θa − 3θe:

θ̇a = ωa + Γa(ψ) + ζa,   θ̇e = ωe + Γe(ψ) + ζe,    ψ̇ = ωa − 3ωe + Γa(ψ) − 3Γe(ψ) + ζ

If ψ̇ never reaches zero there is no full lock, but ψ crawls through a "slow region" and piles up there: statistical 3 : 1 phase locking. The paper finds ωψ/2π = 0.332 cycles/year and a modulation range ΔΓψ/2π = 0.278, so ψ takes 2.3 years per cycle at its fastest and 4.9 at its slowest, with a sharp pile-up near ψ/2π ≈ 0.75. Other ratios (2 : 1, 4 : 1, 5 : 1, 5 : 2) show no such lock. An El Niño's length is the time θe takes to run from 13π/8 to 2π + 3π/8 (La Niña: 3π/8 to 9π/8), found by simulating the noisy equations 5,000 times for each starting month.

How to use it

  • Start with the paper's values: the top-left curve dips but stays above zero, so ψ never stops; the histogram piles up near ψ/2π ≈ 0.75, where ψ moves slowest. That pile-up is the statistical 3 : 1 lock.
  • Read the bottom chart: El Niño (orange) lasts longest when it starts in May–July and shortest in winter; La Niña (blue) peaks around June–August. The shaded ranges are widest for spring starts, as in the paper. The black dots are the paper's reported means.
  • Turn up the seasonal nudge: past about 2.4× the curve touches zero and ENSO locks fully to a three-year beat. At 0 the season has no effect, durations no longer depend on the month, and the histogram goes flat.
  • Change ENSO's natural rate: moving ωe/2π towards 1/3 cycle per year (a three-year ENSO) makes locking easy; moving it away weakens the pile-up.
  • Change the noise: more weather noise widens the duration ranges and blurs the histogram; with no noise every run of the same month gives the same length.
